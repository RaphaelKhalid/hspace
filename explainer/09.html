Level 9: The Hessian

The gradient says which way is uphill. The Hessian says how that answer changes as you walk: bending along each direction, and how the directions interact.

By the end of this level you can
  • build the Hessian as the Jacobian of the gradient, and give each entry its unit (“bushels per bucket per bag”);
  • compute the curvature along any direction with the quadratic form \(\mathbf{v}^\top H\mathbf{v}\);
  • find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of a 2×2 symmetric matrix by hand, and say why they are the principal curvatures;
  • tell a bowl from a dome from a saddle using signs of eigenvalues, or the trace and determinant;
  • show that interaction depends on the axes you choose, and that the eigenvectors are the axes with no interaction;
  • compute the Hessian of a product of two reads, \(\operatorname{sym}(\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^\top)\), and its eigenvalues;
  • measure a Hessian's size (Frobenius norm) and explain which of its features survive a change of units (Sylvester's law of inertia).

Prerequisites: the farm, partial derivatives and the gradient (Level 5), the mixed partial and the 2×2 interaction (Level 6), matrices, transpose and determinant (Level 7), and the Jacobian (Level 8).

9.1 The Hessian is the Jacobian of the gradient

Back to the farm. Let \(W\) be buckets of water, \(F\) bags of fertiliser, and \(Y\) the yield in bushels:

$$ Y(W, F) = 2W + 3F + 0.5\,WF - 0.25\,W^2 - 0.5\,F^2. $$

At Level 5 we found the gradient, the vector of the two partial derivatives:

$$ \nabla Y = \left(\frac{\partial Y}{\partial W},\ \frac{\partial Y}{\partial F}\right) = \big(2 + 0.5F - 0.5W,\ \ 3 + 0.5W - F\big). $$

The gradient depends on the plan. So it is itself a map: two numbers in (\(W, F\)), two numbers out (the two rates). Call this map \(\mathbf{g}(W, F) = \nabla Y(W, F)\). It is a vector-valued function, and Level 8 tells us how to take its derivative: build its Jacobian, one row per output, one column per input.

$$ J_{\mathbf{g}} = \begin{pmatrix} \dfrac{\partial}{\partial W}\Big(\dfrac{\partial Y}{\partial W}\Big) & \dfrac{\partial}{\partial F}\Big(\dfrac{\partial Y}{\partial W}\Big) \\[3ex] \dfrac{\partial}{\partial W}\Big(\dfrac{\partial Y}{\partial F}\Big) & \dfrac{\partial}{\partial F}\Big(\dfrac{\partial Y}{\partial F}\Big) \end{pmatrix}. $$

Compute each entry from the formulas for the two rates, one entry per step. In every step, go term by term: a constant gives 0, a term without the moving input gives 0, and a number times the moving input gives that number.

Step 1: \(\partial/\partial W\) of \(2 + 0.5F - 0.5W\). 2 gives 0. \(0.5F\) has no \(W\), so 0. \(-0.5W\) gives \(-0.5\). Entry: \(-0.5\).

Step 2: \(\partial/\partial F\) of \(2 + 0.5F - 0.5W\). 2 gives 0. \(0.5F\) gives \(0.5\). \(-0.5W\) has no \(F\), so 0. Entry: \(0.5\).

Step 3: \(\partial/\partial W\) of \(3 + 0.5W - F\). 3 gives 0. \(0.5W\) gives \(0.5\). \(-F\) has no \(W\), so 0. Entry: \(0.5\).

Step 4: \(\partial/\partial F\) of \(3 + 0.5W - F\). 3 gives 0. \(0.5W\) has no \(F\), so 0. \(-F\) gives \(-1\). Entry: \(-1\).

Section 9.2 checks all four with real numbers.

$$ H = \nabla^2 Y = \begin{pmatrix} \dfrac{\partial^2 Y}{\partial W^2} & \dfrac{\partial^2 Y}{\partial W\,\partial F} \\[2ex] \dfrac{\partial^2 Y}{\partial F\,\partial W} & \dfrac{\partial^2 Y}{\partial F^2} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -0.5 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & -1 \end{pmatrix}. $$
The Hessian For a function \(f\) with one output and \(d\) inputs \(x_1, \dots, x_d\), the Hessian is the \(d\times d\) matrix of all second partial derivatives, \(H_{ij} = \partial^2 f/\partial x_i\,\partial x_j\). It is the Jacobian of the gradient. Diagonal entries are curvatures along the input axes (Level 3). Off-diagonal entries are interactions (Level 6).

Units of every entry

Each entry is “a rate of a rate”. Its unit is the output unit divided by the units of both inputs involved.

Because \(Y\) is quadratic, these numbers are the same at every plan. For other functions the Hessian changes from point to point, like the Jacobian did at Level 8, and you must say where it is evaluated.

Intuition pump: the slope arrows on a hillside Paint, at every spot on a hillside, an arrow pointing uphill whose length is the steepness. That field of arrows is the gradient. The Hessian answers: if I take one step east, how does the arrow under my feet change? And one step north? Four numbers, because the arrow has two components and you can step in two directions.

9.2 Mechanics: each column is a change of the gradient

Stand at the plan \((W, F) = (4, 2)\). The gradient there is

$$ \nabla Y(4, 2) = (2 + 1 - 2,\ 3 + 2 - 2) = (1, 3) \quad \text{(bushels per bucket, bushels per bag)}. $$

Add one bucket, keep 2 bags. At \((5, 2)\): \(\nabla Y = (2 + 1 - 2.5,\ 3 + 2.5 - 2) = (0.5, 3.5)\). The change is \((0.5 - 1,\ 3.5 - 3) = (-0.5, +0.5)\): column 1 of \(H\). Water got less valuable (diminishing returns), fertiliser got more valuable (the interaction).

Add one bag, keep 4 buckets. At \((4, 3)\): \(\nabla Y = (2 + 1.5 - 2,\ 3 + 2 - 3) = (1.5, 2)\). The change is \((+0.5, -1)\): column 2 of \(H\).

Add one of each, \(\mathbf{v} = (1, 1)\). At \((5, 3)\): \(\nabla Y = (2 + 1.5 - 2.5,\ 3 + 2.5 - 3) = (1, 2.5)\). The change is \((0, -0.5)\). And the matrix-vector product predicts

$$ H\mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} -0.5\times1 + 0.5\times1 \\ 0.5\times1 + (-1)\times1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ -0.5 \end{pmatrix}. $$

Exactly right. \(H\mathbf{v}\) is the change of the gradient when you move along \(\mathbf{v}\). Here it is exact because the gradient of a quadratic is a straight-line function; in general it is the first-order prediction, like \(J\mathbf{v}\) at Level 8. This product is the Hessian-vector product, the workhorse of Level 11.

9.3 Symmetry

The two off-diagonal entries are equal: \(H_{12} = H_{21} = 0.5\). That is the symmetry of mixed partials from Level 6: for a smooth function, the order of the two derivatives does not matter. The units agree too: bushels per bucket per bag equals bushels per bag per bucket. A matrix equal to its own transpose, \(H^\top = H\), is a symmetric matrix.

Symmetry is not a detail. It guarantees three things that the rest of this level uses (the spectral theorem):

  1. the eigenvalues are real numbers;
  2. eigenvectors for different eigenvalues are perpendicular;
  3. there is a full set of them: \(d\) perpendicular unit eigenvectors for a \(d\times d\) matrix.

A practical use: if code returns a “Hessian” that is not symmetric (beyond rounding), something is wrong.

9.4 Curvature along a direction: the quadratic form

Taylor's formula with two inputs

At Level 4, one input: \(f(x + \Delta x) \approx f(x) + f'(x)\Delta x + \tfrac12 f''(x)\Delta x^2\). With two inputs, the slope becomes the gradient and the second derivative becomes the Hessian. For a move \(\Delta\mathbf{x} = (\Delta W, \Delta F)\):

$$ Y(\mathbf{x} + \Delta\mathbf{x}) \approx Y(\mathbf{x}) + \nabla Y\cdot\Delta\mathbf{x} + \tfrac12\,\Delta\mathbf{x}^\top H\,\Delta\mathbf{x}. $$

The last term is a quadratic form. Write it out for a symmetric 2×2 matrix \(\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ b & c \end{pmatrix}\) and any \(\mathbf{v} = (v_1, v_2)\), step by step. The worked numbers use the farm, \(a = -0.5\), \(b = 0.5\), \(c = -1\), with \(\mathbf{v} = (1, 1)\).

Step 1: multiply the matrix by \(\mathbf{v}\), row by row.

$$ H\mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} a v_1 + b v_2 \\ b v_1 + c v_2 \end{pmatrix}. $$

Example: \((-0.5 + 0.5,\ 0.5 - 1) = (0, -0.5)\).

Step 2: dot the result with \(\mathbf{v}\). \(\mathbf{v}^\top\) is \(\mathbf{v}\) as a row, so this is a dot product:

$$ \mathbf{v}^\top H\mathbf{v} = v_1(a v_1 + b v_2) + v_2(b v_1 + c v_2). $$

Example: \(1\times0 + 1\times(-0.5) = -0.5\).

Step 3: multiply out the brackets. \(v_1(a v_1 + b v_2) = a v_1^2 + b\,v_1v_2\) and \(v_2(b v_1 + c v_2) = b\,v_1v_2 + c\,v_2^2\).

Step 4: collect the two \(b\,v_1v_2\) terms. They are equal (because \(H_{12} = H_{21} = b\)), so they add to \(2b\,v_1v_2\):

$$ \mathbf{v}^\top H\mathbf{v} = a v_1^2 + 2b\,v_1v_2 + c\,v_2^2. $$

Example: \(-0.5\times1 + 2\times0.5\times1\times1 - 1\times1 = -0.5 + 1 - 1 = -0.5\), the same as Step 2.

Three terms: curvature along axis 1, twice the interaction, curvature along axis 2. For the farm with \(\mathbf{v} = (\Delta W, \Delta F)\), each term is in bushels: (bushels per bucket²) × bucket², (bushels per bucket per bag) × bucket × bag, (bushels per bag²) × bag².

Worked example: predicting a small change

From \((4, 2)\), where \(Y = 8 + 6 + 4 - 4 - 2 = 12\) bushels, move \(\Delta\mathbf{x} = (0.2, -0.05)\).

  • First-order term: \(\nabla Y\cdot\Delta\mathbf{x} = 1\times0.2 + 3\times(-0.05) = 0.2 - 0.15 = 0.05\) bushels.
  • Second-order term: \(\Delta\mathbf{x}^\top H\Delta\mathbf{x} = -0.5\times0.04 + 2\times0.5\times0.2\times(-0.05) - 1\times0.0025 = -0.02 - 0.01 - 0.0025 = -0.0325\); half of it is \(-0.01625\) bushels.
  • Prediction: \(12 + 0.05 - 0.01625 = 12.03375\) bushels.
  • Exact: \(Y(4.2, 1.95) = 8.4 + 5.85 + 4.095 - 4.41 - 1.90125 = 12.03375\) bushels.

An exact match, because \(Y\) has no terms beyond second order. The first-order (Jacobian) prediction alone, 12.05, was off by the curvature term.

The curvature along a line

Walk from \(\mathbf{x}\) along a direction \(\mathbf{v}\): the plans \(\mathbf{x} + t\mathbf{v}\). The yield along this walk is a function of one number \(t\).

Step 1: put \(\Delta\mathbf{x} = t\mathbf{v}\) in Taylor's formula. The first-order term is \(\nabla Y\cdot(t\mathbf{v}) = t\,(\nabla Y\cdot\mathbf{v})\). In the second-order term each of the two copies of \(t\mathbf{v}\) brings out one \(t\): \((t\mathbf{v})^\top H(t\mathbf{v}) = t^2\,\mathbf{v}^\top H\mathbf{v}\). So

$$ Y(\mathbf{x} + t\mathbf{v}) = Y(\mathbf{x}) + t\,(\nabla Y\cdot\mathbf{v}) + \tfrac12\,t^2\,(\mathbf{v}^\top H\mathbf{v}). $$

The two brackets are fixed numbers; only \(t\) varies.

Step 2: differentiate once in \(t\). \(Y(\mathbf{x})\) gives 0. \(t\,(\nabla Y\cdot\mathbf{v})\) gives \(\nabla Y\cdot\mathbf{v}\). By the power rule, \(\tfrac12 t^2\) gives \(t\), so the last term gives \(t\,(\mathbf{v}^\top H\mathbf{v})\).

Step 3: differentiate again. \(\nabla Y\cdot\mathbf{v}\) is a constant, so it gives 0. \(t\,(\mathbf{v}^\top H\mathbf{v})\) gives \(\mathbf{v}^\top H\mathbf{v}\).

So the second derivative in \(t\) is \(\mathbf{v}^\top H\mathbf{v}\). So the quadratic form is the curvature you feel when walking along \(\mathbf{v}\). For a unit direction \(\hat{\mathbf{v}}\) we call \(\hat{\mathbf{v}}^\top H\hat{\mathbf{v}}\) the curvature along that direction, in bushels per unit step².

Mechanics at the best plan. The best plan is \((14, 10)\), where both rates are zero: \(2 + 5 - 7 = 0\) and \(3 + 7 - 10 = 0\), and \(Y = 29\) bushels. Walk along \(\mathbf{v} = (1, 1)\): \(\mathbf{v}^\top H\mathbf{v} = -0.5 + 2\times0.5 - 1 = -0.5\), so \(Y(14 + t, 10 + t) = 29 - 0.25t^2\). Check at \(t = 2\): \(Y(16, 12) = 32 + 36 + 96 - 64 - 72 = 28 = 29 - 0.25\times4\). For the unit direction \((1,1)/\sqrt2\) the curvature is \(-0.5/2 = -0.25\) bushels per unit step².

9.5 Eigenvectors and eigenvalues: the principal curvatures

A direction \(\mathbf{u}\) is an eigenvector of \(H\) if \(H\) does not turn it, only stretches it:

$$ H\mathbf{u} = \lambda\,\mathbf{u}. $$

The stretch factor \(\lambda\) (“lambda”) is the eigenvalue. Then the curvature along a unit eigenvector is \(\mathbf{u}^\top H\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{u}^\top(\lambda\mathbf{u}) = \lambda\,\mathbf{u}^\top\mathbf{u} = \lambda\). An eigenvalue is the curvature along its eigenvector.

Finding them for a 2×2 symmetric matrix

Step 1: move everything to one side. \(H\mathbf{u} = \lambda\mathbf{u}\) is the same as \(H\mathbf{u} - \lambda I\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0}\), because \(I\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{u}\). Take out \(\mathbf{u}\): \((H - \lambda I)\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0}\), with \(\mathbf{u} \ne \mathbf{0}\).

Step 2: require determinant zero. A non-zero vector is squashed to zero only if \(H - \lambda I\) squashes the plane flat, that is, has determinant zero (Level 7). Subtracting \(\lambda I\) takes \(\lambda\) off each diagonal entry. Apply \(ad - bc\):

$$ \det\begin{pmatrix} a - \lambda & b \\ b & c - \lambda \end{pmatrix} = (a - \lambda)(c - \lambda) - b^2 = 0. $$

Step 3: multiply out the bracket. \((a - \lambda)(c - \lambda) = ac - a\lambda - c\lambda + \lambda^2\). Collect the \(\lambda\) terms and put the highest power first:

$$ \lambda^2 - (a + c)\lambda + (ac - b^2) = 0. $$

This is the characteristic equation.

Step 4: put in the farm numbers. \(a = -0.5\), \(b = 0.5\), \(c = -1\). Then \(a + c = -1.5\) and \(ac - b^2 = 0.5 - 0.25 = 0.25\):

$$ \lambda^2 - (-1.5)\lambda + 0.25 = \lambda^2 + 1.5\lambda + 0.25 = 0. $$

Step 5: the quadratic formula. For an equation “\(\lambda^2\) + (first number) × \(\lambda\) + (second number) = 0”, the two solutions are \(\lambda = \big(-\text{first} \pm \sqrt{\text{first}^2 - 4\times\text{second}}\big)/2\). Here the first number is 1.5 and the second is 0.25:

$$ \lambda = \frac{-1.5 \pm \sqrt{1.5^2 - 4\times0.25}}{2} = \frac{-1.5 \pm \sqrt{2.25 - 1}}{2} = \frac{-1.5 \pm \sqrt{1.25}}{2} = \frac{-1.5 \pm 1.1180}{2}. $$ $$ \lambda_1 = \frac{-0.3820}{2} = -0.1910, \qquad \lambda_2 = \frac{-2.6180}{2} = -1.3090 \quad \text{(bushels per unit step}^2\text{)}. $$

The eigenvectors. Find the direction for each \(\lambda\) in three steps.

Step 1: read the first row. The first row of \((H - \lambda I)\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0}\) reads \((a - \lambda)u_1 + b\,u_2 = 0\).

Step 2: move the \(u_1\) term across. Subtract \((a - \lambda)u_1\) from both sides: \(b\,u_2 = (\lambda - a)\,u_1\).

Step 3: divide both sides by \(b\,u_1\). \(u_2/u_1 = (\lambda - a)/b\). Only the ratio is fixed, because any multiple of an eigenvector is also one.

  • For \(\lambda_1 = -0.1910\): \(u_2/u_1 = (-0.1910 + 0.5)/0.5 = 0.6180\). Take \((1, 0.6180)\), length \(\sqrt{1 + 0.3820} = 1.1756\), so \(\mathbf{u}_1 = (0.8507, 0.5257)\). Its angle is \(31.72^\circ\) from the water axis.
  • For \(\lambda_2 = -1.3090\): \(u_2/u_1 = (-1.3090 + 0.5)/0.5 = -1.6180\). Take \((1, -1.6180)\), length \(1.9021\), so \(\mathbf{u}_2 = (0.5257, -0.8507)\), or its negative \((-0.5257, 0.8507)\) at \(121.72^\circ\). Either sign is the same direction line.

Checks. Perpendicular: \(0.8507\times(-0.5257) + 0.5257\times0.8507 = 0\). Eigen-equation: \(H\mathbf{u}_1 = (-0.5\times0.8507 + 0.5\times0.5257,\ 0.5\times0.8507 - 0.5257) = (-0.1625, -0.1004)\), and \(\lambda_1\mathbf{u}_1 = -0.1910\times(0.8507, 0.5257) = (-0.1625, -0.1004)\). Equal.

Why they are the extremes

Do it one operation at a time. The worked numbers use the 45° direction \(\hat{\mathbf{v}} = (0.7071, 0.7071)\), whose curvature the table above gives as −0.25.

Step 1: write the direction in eigenvector coordinates. \(\hat{\mathbf{v}} = p\,\mathbf{u}_1 + q\,\mathbf{u}_2\). Because \(\mathbf{u}_1\) and \(\mathbf{u}_2\) are perpendicular unit vectors, \(p = \hat{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\mathbf{u}_1\), \(q = \hat{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\mathbf{u}_2\) and \(p^2 + q^2 = 1\) (Level 7, 7.9). Example: \(p = 0.7071\times(0.8507 + 0.5257) = 0.9732\) and \(q = 0.7071\times(0.5257 - 0.8507) = -0.2298\); \(p^2 = 0.9472\), \(q^2 = 0.0528\), sum 1.

Step 2: multiply out. Put \(p\,\mathbf{u}_1 + q\,\mathbf{u}_2\) in both places of \(\hat{\mathbf{v}}^\top H\hat{\mathbf{v}}\) and multiply every term by every term, as with brackets of numbers. Four terms come out:

$$ \hat{\mathbf{v}}^\top H\hat{\mathbf{v}} = p^2\,\mathbf{u}_1^\top H\mathbf{u}_1 + pq\,\mathbf{u}_1^\top H\mathbf{u}_2 + qp\,\mathbf{u}_2^\top H\mathbf{u}_1 + q^2\,\mathbf{u}_2^\top H\mathbf{u}_2 . $$

Step 3: use the eigen-equation in each term. \(H\mathbf{u}_1 = \lambda_1\mathbf{u}_1\) and \(H\mathbf{u}_2 = \lambda_2\mathbf{u}_2\). So \(\mathbf{u}_1^\top H\mathbf{u}_1 = \lambda_1\,\mathbf{u}_1\cdot\mathbf{u}_1 = \lambda_1\) (a unit vector dotted with itself is 1), and likewise \(\mathbf{u}_2^\top H\mathbf{u}_2 = \lambda_2\).

Step 4: the cross terms vanish. \(\mathbf{u}_1^\top H\mathbf{u}_2 = \mathbf{u}_1^\top(\lambda_2\mathbf{u}_2) = \lambda_2(\mathbf{u}_1\cdot\mathbf{u}_2) = 0\), because the eigenvectors are perpendicular. The same holds for \(\mathbf{u}_2^\top H\mathbf{u}_1\). So

$$ \hat{\mathbf{v}}^\top H\hat{\mathbf{v}} = p^2\lambda_1 + q^2\lambda_2 . $$

Example: \(0.9472\times(-0.1910) + 0.0528\times(-1.3090) = -0.1809 - 0.0691 = -0.25\), the table's value.

What is left is a weighted average of \(\lambda_1\) and \(\lambda_2\) with weights \(p^2 + q^2 = 1\). So every direction's curvature lies between \(\lambda_2\) and \(\lambda_1\), and the extremes are reached exactly at the eigenvectors. These two curvatures are the principal curvatures.

Units warning: eigenvectors need comparable steps A direction like \(\mathbf{u}_1 = (0.8507, 0.5257)\) mixes buckets and bags, and “length 1” added buckets² to bags². That only makes sense because we decided one bucket and one bag count as equal-sized steps. Measure water in litres and the eigenvectors move. Section 9.10 shows by how much, and what does not move.

Farm reading. The gentlest direction, \(\mathbf{u}_1\), adds water and fertiliser together, about 1.618 buckets per bag. Along it, yield falls off slowly from the peak (\(-0.191\)). The steepest direction, \(\mathbf{u}_2\), trades one input for the other. Along it, yield falls off fast (\(-1.309\)). Check from the peak: \(Y\big((14, 10) + 2\mathbf{u}_1\big) = 28.618 = 29 + \tfrac12(-0.1910)(2^2)\).

9.6 Bowl, dome, saddle: definiteness

Take the pure quadratic surface \(z = \tfrac12\mathbf{x}^\top H\mathbf{x}\). In eigenvector coordinates it is \(z = \tfrac12(\lambda_1 p^2 + \lambda_2 q^2)\): a sum of two parabolas at right angles, no cross term. The signs of the two eigenvalues decide the shape.

EigenvaluesShapeNameReal-world picture
both \(\gt 0\)bowl: up in every directionpositive definitea salad bowl, a valley floor
both \(\lt 0\)dome: down in every directionnegative definitea hilltop; the farm at its best plan
one \(\gt 0\), one \(\lt 0\)saddle: up one way, down the otherindefinitea mountain pass, a horse saddle

The same statements in terms of the quadratic form: \(H\) is positive definite if \(\mathbf{v}^\top H\mathbf{v} \gt 0\) for every \(\mathbf{v} \ne \mathbf{0}\); negative definite if \(\lt 0\) for every such \(\mathbf{v}\); indefinite if it takes both signs; positive semidefinite if it is never negative. Since the farm's eigenvalues are both negative, its Hessian is negative definite: every direction curves down, which is why \((14, 10)\) is a maximum and not a pass.

9.7 The second-derivative test, trace and determinant

Two shortcuts avoid solving for the eigenvalues. Derive them in three steps.

Step 1: write the characteristic polynomial in factored form. Its two roots are \(\lambda_1\) and \(\lambda_2\). A polynomial that starts with \(\lambda^2\) and is zero exactly at \(\lambda_1\) and \(\lambda_2\) is \((\lambda - \lambda_1)(\lambda - \lambda_2)\):

$$ \lambda^2 - (a + c)\lambda + (ac - b^2) = (\lambda - \lambda_1)(\lambda - \lambda_2). $$

Step 2: multiply out the right side. \((\lambda - \lambda_1)(\lambda - \lambda_2) = \lambda^2 - \lambda_2\lambda - \lambda_1\lambda + \lambda_1\lambda_2 = \lambda^2 - (\lambda_1 + \lambda_2)\lambda + \lambda_1\lambda_2\).

$$ \lambda^2 - (a + c)\lambda + (ac - b^2) = \lambda^2 - (\lambda_1 + \lambda_2)\lambda + \lambda_1\lambda_2. $$

Step 3: match the coefficients. The two sides are equal for every \(\lambda\), so the numbers in front of \(\lambda\) must match, and so must the plain numbers:

$$ \operatorname{tr} H = a + c = \lambda_1 + \lambda_2, \qquad \det H = ac - b^2 = \lambda_1\lambda_2. $$

Farm check: \(\operatorname{tr} H = -0.5 - 1 = -1.5 = -0.1910 - 1.3090\), and \(\det H = 0.5 - 0.25 = 0.25 = (-0.1910)\times(-1.3090)\).

Second-derivative test in two dimensions At a critical point (gradient zero):
\(\det H \gt 0\) and \(\operatorname{tr} H \lt 0\): both eigenvalues negative: a maximum.
\(\det H \gt 0\) and \(\operatorname{tr} H \gt 0\): both positive: a minimum.
\(\det H \lt 0\): opposite signs: a saddle.
\(\det H = 0\): the test says nothing; look at higher-order terms.

Why: a positive product means the signs agree, and then the sum tells you which sign. A negative product means the signs differ. (With \(\det H \gt 0\), the sign of \(a\) alone also works, since \(ac \gt b^2 \ge 0\) forces \(a\) and \(c\) to share a sign.)

Mechanics: turn up the interaction. Keep \(a = -0.5\) and \(c = -1\), and change only the interaction \(b\) (the coefficient of \(WF\) in \(Y\)). Then \(\det H = 0.5 - b^2\).

\(b\) (bushels per bucket per bag)\(\det H\)shape at the critical point

In farm words: if water and fertiliser helped each other strongly enough, adding both together would gain more from the interaction than diminishing returns take away. Yield would grow without limit along the ridge, and there would be no best plan. Of course, a real farm would hit other limits; the toy only shows what the numbers say.

A note on units. \(\det H\) has unit bushels² per bucket² per bag², and its sign does not depend on how you measure water or fertiliser. \(\operatorname{tr} H\) adds bushels per bucket² to bushels per bag², so its size depends on the step units you choose. The test above only uses the trace's sign when \(\det H \gt 0\), where it agrees with the sign of \(a\), so the verdict never depends on units.

9.8 Interaction depends on the axes: rotate it away

At Level 6, the interaction between water and fertiliser was the mixed partial, 0.5 bushels per bucket per bag. But “water” and “fertiliser” are just the two axes we happened to pick. Pick two other perpendicular directions and the interaction between them is different.

Rotate the axes by an angle \(\varphi\). The new axes are the columns of

$$ R = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\varphi & -\sin\varphi \\ \sin\varphi & \cos\varphi \end{pmatrix}, $$

and the Hessian written in the new axes is \(H' = R^\top HR\). Multiply it out, one entry at a time. The worked numbers use the farm and \(\varphi = 45^\circ\), where \(\cos\varphi = \sin\varphi = 0.7071\) and \(\cos^2\varphi = \sin^2\varphi = \sin\varphi\cos\varphi = 0.5\).

Step 1: what each entry is. Row \(i\) of \(R^\top\) is column \(i\) of \(R\). So entry \((i, j)\) of \(R^\top HR\) is (column \(i\) of \(R\)) dotted with \(H\) times (column \(j\) of \(R\)). Column 1 is \((\cos\varphi, \sin\varphi)\) and column 2 is \((-\sin\varphi, \cos\varphi)\).

Step 2: \(H'_{11}\) is the quadratic form along column 1. Put \(v_1 = \cos\varphi\), \(v_2 = \sin\varphi\) into \(a v_1^2 + 2b\,v_1v_2 + c\,v_2^2\) from 9.4:

$$ H'_{11} = a\cos^2\varphi + 2b\sin\varphi\cos\varphi + c\sin^2\varphi . $$

Example: \(-0.5\times0.5 + 2\times0.5\times0.5 - 1\times0.5 = -0.25 + 0.5 - 0.5 = -0.25\), the 45° row of the table in 9.4.

Step 3: \(H'_{22}\) is the quadratic form along column 2. Put \(v_1 = -\sin\varphi\), \(v_2 = \cos\varphi\). The middle term picks up the minus sign:

$$ H'_{22} = a\sin^2\varphi - 2b\sin\varphi\cos\varphi + c\cos^2\varphi . $$

Example: \(-0.25 - 0.5 - 0.5 = -1.25\), the 135° row of that table.

Step 4: \(H'_{12}\), first multiply \(H\) by column 2. Row by row:

$$ H\begin{pmatrix} -\sin\varphi \\ \cos\varphi \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -a\sin\varphi + b\cos\varphi \\ -b\sin\varphi + c\cos\varphi \end{pmatrix}. $$

Example: \((0.3536 + 0.3536,\ -0.3536 - 0.7071) = (0.7071, -1.0607)\).

Step 5: then dot with column 1. Multiply the first entry by \(\cos\varphi\), the second by \(\sin\varphi\), and add:

$$ H'_{12} = -a\sin\varphi\cos\varphi + b\cos^2\varphi - b\sin^2\varphi + c\sin\varphi\cos\varphi . $$

Example: \(0.7071\times0.7071 + (-1.0607)\times0.7071 = 0.5 - 0.75 = -0.25\).

Step 6: collect. The \(a\) and \(c\) terms share \(\sin\varphi\cos\varphi\); the \(b\) terms share \(b\):

$$ H'_{12} = (c - a)\sin\varphi\cos\varphi + b(\cos^2\varphi - \sin^2\varphi). $$

Step 7: use the double-angle rules. \(\sin\varphi\cos\varphi = \tfrac12\sin 2\varphi\) and \(\cos^2\varphi - \sin^2\varphi = \cos 2\varphi\) (standard trigonometry; check at 45°: \(0.5 = \tfrac12\sin 90^\circ\) and \(0 = \cos 90^\circ\)):

$$ H'_{12} = \tfrac12(c - a)\sin 2\varphi + b\cos 2\varphi. $$

Example: \(\tfrac12(-1 + 0.5)\times1 + 0.5\times0 = -0.25\). At 45° the new axes still interact.

Step 8: find where the interaction is zero. Set \(H'_{12} = 0\) and move the first term across: \(b\cos 2\varphi = \tfrac12(a - c)\sin 2\varphi\). Divide both sides by \(\tfrac12(a - c)\cos 2\varphi\):

$$ \tan 2\varphi = \frac{\sin 2\varphi}{\cos 2\varphi} = \frac{2b}{a - c}. $$

For the farm, \(2\times0.5/(-0.5 + 1) = 1/0.5 = 2\), so \(2\varphi = 63.43^\circ\) (the angle whose tangent is 2) and \(\varphi = 31.72^\circ\): exactly the angle of the eigenvector \(\mathbf{u}_1\). In these axes,

$$ H' = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda_1 & 0 \\ 0 & \lambda_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -0.1910 & 0 \\ 0 & -1.3090 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad Y = 29 + \tfrac12\lambda_1 p^2 + \tfrac12\lambda_2 q^2 $$

where \(p, q\) are the move away from \((14, 10)\) along \(\mathbf{u}_1, \mathbf{u}_2\). No \(pq\) term: no interaction.

Mechanics: the 2×2 test along the eigenvectors

Repeat Level 6's four-corner test from \(\mathbf{x} = (4, 2)\), but move one unit step along \(\mathbf{u}_1\) and one along \(\mathbf{u}_2' = (-0.5257, 0.8507)\) instead of one bucket and one bag:

$$ Y(\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{u}_1 + \mathbf{u}_2') - Y(\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{u}_1) - Y(\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{u}_2') + Y(\mathbf{x}) = 15.7041 - 14.3324 - 13.3717 + 12 = 0.0000. $$

With bucket and bag moves the same test gave 0.5 bushels (Level 6). Same function, same starting plan, different axes, different interaction.

Interaction is a property of a pair of directions The off-diagonal entry \(H_{12}\) is the interaction between input axis 1 and input axis 2. Rotate the axes and it changes. The eigenvectors are the unique perpendicular axes (when the eigenvalues differ) along which there is no interaction at all: all of the bending is pure curvature.
Intuition pump: a rugby ball lying at a slant Lay a rugby ball on a table at a slant and slice it horizontally. The slice is a tilted ellipse. Described in the table's north-south and east-west axes, the ellipse needs a cross term. Described along its own long and short axes, it does not. The function did not change; only the description did.

9.9 Products make simple curvature: \(\operatorname{sym}(\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^\top)\)

Real-world example: the area of a field

A rectangular field is \(x_1\) metres wide and \(x_2\) metres long. Its area is \(f = x_1 x_2\) m². At a 10 m × 10 m field: grow both sides by 1 m and the area goes 100 → 121 (gain 21). Grow one and shrink the other by 1 m: 100 → 99 (loss 1). Build the Hessian in three steps.

Step 1: first derivatives. In \(x_1\), the factor \(x_2\) is a constant multiplier, so \(\partial f/\partial x_1 = x_2\). Likewise \(\partial f/\partial x_2 = x_1\).

Step 2: second derivatives. \(x_2\) has no \(x_1\) in it, so \(\partial^2 f/\partial x_1^2 = 0\); in \(x_2\) it gives 1, so \(\partial^2 f/\partial x_1\,\partial x_2 = 1\). Likewise \(\partial^2 f/\partial x_2^2 = 0\).

$$ \nabla^2(x_1x_2) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \quad \text{(m}^2\text{ per m per m, a pure number)}. $$

Step 3: eigenvalues. Here \(a = 0\), \(b = 1\), \(c = 0\). The characteristic equation of 9.5 is \(\lambda^2 - 0\times\lambda + (0 - 1) = \lambda^2 - 1 = 0\), so \(\lambda = \pm1\). For \(+1\): \(u_2/u_1 = (1 - 0)/1 = 1\), direction \((1, 1)\). For \(-1\): \(u_2/u_1 = -1\), direction \((1, -1)\).

Eigenvalues \(\pm1\), along \((1, 1)\) and \((1, -1)\): a saddle. Along \((1, 1)\): \(f(10 + t, 10 + t) = 100 + 20t + t^2\), second derivative \(2 = \mathbf{v}^\top H\mathbf{v}\) for \(\mathbf{v} = (1, 1)\). Along \((1, -1)\): \(100 - t^2\), second derivative \(-2\).

The general product of two reads

Let \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\) be two fixed directions, and let \(f\) multiply two readings of the input \(\mathbf{x}\):

$$ f(\mathbf{x}) = (\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{x})(\mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{x}). $$

(The field is the case \(\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{e}_1 = (1, 0)\), \(\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{e}_2 = (0, 1)\).) First derivatives, by the product rule: \(\partial(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{x})/\partial x_i = a_i\), so

$$ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i} = a_i\,(\mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{x}) + b_i\,(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{x}). $$

Second derivatives: differentiate again by \(x_j\). The factors \(a_i\) and \(b_i\) are constants, and \(\partial(\mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{x})/\partial x_j = b_j\):

$$ H_{ij} = a_i b_j + b_i a_j, \qquad H = \mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^\top + \mathbf{b}\mathbf{a}^\top = \operatorname{sym}(\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^\top). $$

Here \(\operatorname{sym}(M) := M + M^\top\), the convention of hlens/paper/theory-v2.md. Many textbooks put a \(\tfrac12\) in front; with that convention the Hessian would be \(2\operatorname{sym}(\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^\top)\). Always check which one a formula uses.

Worked example. \(\mathbf{a} = (2, 1)\), \(\mathbf{b} = (1, 3)\). First directly, then by the formula.

Step 1: write out the two reads. \(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{x} = 2x_1 + x_2\) and \(\mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{x} = x_1 + 3x_2\), so \(f = (2x_1 + x_2)(x_1 + 3x_2)\).

Step 2: multiply out. Every term by every term: \(2x_1\cdot x_1 + 2x_1\cdot 3x_2 + x_2\cdot x_1 + x_2\cdot 3x_2 = 2x_1^2 + 6x_1x_2 + x_1x_2 + 3x_2^2 = 2x_1^2 + 7x_1x_2 + 3x_2^2\).

Step 3: read off the second derivatives. \(2x_1^2\) differentiated twice in \(x_1\) gives \(2\times2 = 4\). \(7x_1x_2\) differentiated once in each gives 7. \(3x_2^2\) differentiated twice in \(x_2\) gives \(3\times2 = 6\). So \(H = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 7 \\ 7 & 6 \end{pmatrix}\).

Step 4: the formula. Column times row puts \(a_ib_j\) in row \(i\), column \(j\): \(\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^\top = \begin{pmatrix} 2\cdot1 & 2\cdot3 \\ 1\cdot1 & 1\cdot3\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 6 \\ 1 & 3\end{pmatrix}\). Its transpose is \(\mathbf{b}\mathbf{a}^\top = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 6 & 3\end{pmatrix}\). Add entry by entry: \(\begin{pmatrix} 2 + 2 & 6 + 1 \\ 1 + 6 & 3 + 3\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 7 \\ 7 & 6\end{pmatrix}\). Same as Step 3.

Its eigenvalues: \(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} \pm \lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert\)

Let \(\hat{\mathbf{a}}, \hat{\mathbf{b}}\) be the unit vectors along \(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}\), and let \(\gamma\) be the angle between them, so \(\cos\gamma = \hat{\mathbf{a}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{b}}\) (Level 7). The worked numbers use the example above: \(\hat{\mathbf{a}} = (2, 1)/\sqrt5 = (0.8944, 0.4472)\), \(\hat{\mathbf{b}} = (1, 3)/\sqrt{10} = (0.3162, 0.9487)\), \(\cos\gamma = 0.7071\).

Step 1: pull out the lengths. \(\mathbf{a} = \lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert\,\hat{\mathbf{a}}\) and \(\mathbf{b} = \lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert\,\hat{\mathbf{b}}\). Each outer product contains one \(\mathbf{a}\) and one \(\mathbf{b}\), so both lengths come out in front:

$$ H = \lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert\,(\hat{\mathbf{a}}\hat{\mathbf{b}}^\top + \hat{\mathbf{b}}\hat{\mathbf{a}}^\top). $$

Step 2: apply the first outer product to \(\hat{\mathbf{a}} + \hat{\mathbf{b}}\). “Read along the right, write along the left” (Level 7): \(\hat{\mathbf{a}}\hat{\mathbf{b}}^\top\) reads \(\hat{\mathbf{b}}\cdot(\hat{\mathbf{a}} + \hat{\mathbf{b}}) = \cos\gamma + 1\) and writes that many copies of \(\hat{\mathbf{a}}\). (A unit vector dotted with itself is 1.)

Step 3: apply the second outer product. \(\hat{\mathbf{b}}\hat{\mathbf{a}}^\top\) reads \(\hat{\mathbf{a}}\cdot(\hat{\mathbf{a}} + \hat{\mathbf{b}}) = 1 + \cos\gamma\) and writes that many copies of \(\hat{\mathbf{b}}\).

Step 4: add, and take out the common number.

$$ (\hat{\mathbf{a}}\hat{\mathbf{b}}^\top + \hat{\mathbf{b}}\hat{\mathbf{a}}^\top)(\hat{\mathbf{a}} + \hat{\mathbf{b}}) = \hat{\mathbf{a}}(\cos\gamma + 1) + \hat{\mathbf{b}}(1 + \cos\gamma) = (1 + \cos\gamma)(\hat{\mathbf{a}} + \hat{\mathbf{b}}). $$

Example: \(\hat{\mathbf{a}} + \hat{\mathbf{b}} = (1.2106, 1.3959)\), and \(1.7071\times(1.2106, 1.3959) = (2.0667, 2.3829)\).

Step 5: put the lengths back. So \(\hat{\mathbf{a}} + \hat{\mathbf{b}}\) is an eigenvector of \(H\) with eigenvalue \(\lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert(1 + \cos\gamma)\). Multiply out, and use \(\lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert\cos\gamma = \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}\) (Level 7): the eigenvalue is \(\lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert + \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}\). The same steps with \(\hat{\mathbf{a}} - \hat{\mathbf{b}}\) give \((\cos\gamma - 1)(\hat{\mathbf{a}} - \hat{\mathbf{b}})\), eigenvalue \(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} - \lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert\). Any direction perpendicular to both \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\) is read as zero by both terms: eigenvalue 0.

For the example: \(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = 5\), \(\lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert = \sqrt5\sqrt{10} = \sqrt{50} = 7.0711\), so \(\lambda = 12.0711\) and \(-2.0711\). Check: sum \(10 = 4 + 6 = \operatorname{tr} H\); product \(-25 = 24 - 49 = \det H\).

What a product of two reads does to curvature \(\operatorname{sym}(\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^\top)\) has rank at most 2, in any dimension. Unless \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\) are parallel it has one positive and one negative eigenvalue (inertia \((1, 1)\)): a saddle in the plane of \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\), flat in every other direction. If \(\mathbf{b}\) is a multiple of \(\mathbf{a}\), the product is a square, \(k(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{x})^2\) for some number \(k\), and the Hessian \(2k\,\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^\top\) has rank 1.

The farm, rebuilt from pieces. Each term of \(Y\) is a product or a square of the two reads \(W = \mathbf{e}_1\cdot\mathbf{x}\) and \(F = \mathbf{e}_2\cdot\mathbf{x}\):

$$ \underbrace{-0.25W^2}_{-0.5\,\mathbf{e}_1\mathbf{e}_1^\top} + \underbrace{0.5\,WF}_{0.5\,\operatorname{sym}(\mathbf{e}_1\mathbf{e}_2^\top)} \underbrace{-\,0.5F^2}_{-1\,\mathbf{e}_2\mathbf{e}_2^\top} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \begin{pmatrix} -0.5 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & 0 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix} = H. $$

The interaction term on its own is a saddle (eigenvalues \(\pm0.5\)). Added to the two downward squares, the total is still a dome, because \(\det H = 0.25 \gt 0\).

9.10 How big is a Hessian, and what survives a change of units

The Frobenius norm

One number for the overall size of a matrix: square every entry, add, take the square root.

$$ \lVert H\rVert_F = \sqrt{(-0.5)^2 + 0.5^2 + 0.5^2 + (-1)^2} = \sqrt{0.25 + 0.25 + 0.25 + 1} = \sqrt{1.75} = 1.3229. $$

For a symmetric matrix this equals \(\sqrt{\lambda_1^2 + \lambda_2^2}\): here \(\sqrt{0.0365 + 1.7135} = \sqrt{1.75}\). The reason: rotating to the eigenvector axes does not change the sum of squared entries (a rotation keeps lengths), and in those axes the only entries are the eigenvalues.

Inertia

The inertia of a symmetric matrix is the count \((n_+, n_-, n_0)\) of positive, negative and zero eigenvalues. The farm: \((0, 2, 0)\). A product of two non-parallel reads: \((1, 1, 0)\) in 2-D, and \((1, 1, d - 2)\) in \(d\) dimensions.

Mechanics: measure water in litres

Let \(w\) be water in litres, so \(W = 0.1w\) buckets. Re-express the yield in \((w, F)\) and differentiate again, one step at a time.

Step 1: one derivative in \(w\). One more litre is 0.1 more bucket. So the yield per litre is 0.1 times the yield per bucket: \(\partial Y/\partial w = 0.1\,\partial Y/\partial W\). (This is the chain rule, previewed here; Level 10 proves it.) Example at \((4, 2)\): \(0.1\times1 = 0.1\) bushels per litre.

Step 2: the second derivative in \(w\). Apply Step 1 again to \(\partial Y/\partial w\). A second factor 0.1 comes out:

$$ \frac{\partial^2 Y}{\partial w^2} = 0.1\times0.1\times\frac{\partial^2 Y}{\partial W^2} = 0.01\times(-0.5) = -0.005\ \tfrac{\text{bushels}}{\text{litre}^2}. $$

Step 3: the mixed entry. Only one of the two derivatives is in \(w\), so only one factor 0.1 comes out:

$$ \frac{\partial^2 Y}{\partial w\,\partial F} = 0.1\times\frac{\partial^2 Y}{\partial W\,\partial F} = 0.1\times0.5 = 0.05\ \tfrac{\text{bushels}}{\text{litre}\cdot\text{bag}}. $$

Step 4: the fertiliser entry. No derivative is in \(w\), so it is unchanged: \(\partial^2 Y/\partial F^2 = -1\) bushels per bag².

Step 5: the same thing as a matrix product. Let \(B = \begin{pmatrix} 0.1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}\) (the machine that turns a move in (litres, bags) into a move in (buckets, bags)). Because \(B\) is diagonal, \(B^\top HB\) multiplies entry \((i, j)\) of \(H\) by \(B_{ii}\times B_{jj}\): \(0.1\times0.1\) for entry (1, 1), \(0.1\times1\) for the off-diagonal entries, \(1\times1\) for entry (2, 2). That is Steps 2 to 4 exactly. So

$$ H_L = B^\top HB = \begin{pmatrix} -0.005 & 0.05 \\ 0.05 & -1 \end{pmatrix}. $$

Step 6: its eigenvalues. Use the characteristic equation of 9.5 with \(a = -0.005\), \(b = 0.05\), \(c = -1\): \(a + c = -1.005\) and \(ac - b^2 = 0.005 - 0.0025 = 0.0025\). So \(\lambda^2 + 1.005\lambda + 0.0025 = 0\), and by the quadratic formula

$$ \lambda = \frac{-1.005 \pm \sqrt{1.005^2 - 4\times0.0025}}{2} = \frac{-1.005 \pm \sqrt{1.000025}}{2} = \frac{-1.005 \pm 1.0000125}{2}, $$

which gives \(\lambda = -0.0025\) and \(\lambda = -1.0025\).

Step 7: the gentlest direction. For \(\lambda = -0.0025\): \(u_2/u_1 = (\lambda - a)/b = (-0.0025 + 0.005)/0.05 = 0.0501\) (to 4 decimals, using the unrounded \(\lambda = -0.0024938\)). Take \((1, 0.0501)\), length \(\sqrt{1 + 0.0025} = 1.0013\), so the unit direction is \((0.9987, 0.0501)\).

The gentlest direction now says 1 bag per 19.95 litres, which is 1 bag per 1.995 buckets, not 1 bag per 1.618 buckets. The eigenvalues changed and the eigenvectors moved. What stayed? Both eigenvalues are still negative: the inertia is still \((0, 2, 0)\), and \(\det H_L = 0.005 - 0.0025 = 0.0025 \gt 0\) still says “maximum”.

Sylvester's law of inertia For any invertible matrix \(B\), the matrices \(H\) and \(B^\top HB\) have the same inertia: the same numbers of positive, negative and zero eigenvalues. A rotation (where \(B^\top B = I\)) keeps even the eigenvalues. A stretch or unit change moves eigenvalues and eigenvectors, but never flips a sign.

Why, at least for definiteness: \(\mathbf{v}^\top(B^\top HB)\mathbf{v} = (B\mathbf{v})^\top H(B\mathbf{v})\). The new matrix tested on \(\mathbf{v}\) gives the same number as the old one tested on the direction \(B\mathbf{v}\). If the old one is negative on every non-zero direction, so is the new one, because \(B\mathbf{v} \ne \mathbf{0}\) whenever \(\mathbf{v} \ne \mathbf{0}\).

9.11 Why this matters for H-space

  1. The H-lens is an averaged Hessian. In hlens/paper/theory-v2.md §0, \(T_\ell\) averages the Hessian of a readout of the final state with respect to the layer-\(\ell\) state, over texts and positions: “the J-lens reduction one derivative higher”. For each readout direction it is a symmetric 5,120 × 5,120 matrix for Qwen3.6-27B. Everything in 9.3 to 9.6 applies: real eigenvalues, perpendicular eigenvectors, principal curvatures.
  2. Products of reads are where curvature comes from. A SwiGLU neuron multiplies two reads of the state (one of them first bent through SiLU). theory-v2 Lemma 2 writes a sublayer's Hessian as a sum of terms \(\operatorname{sym}(\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^\top)\) (product sites) and \(\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^\top\) times a number (threshold sites). Remark 2.1 states that a product site has eigenvalues of the form \(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} \pm \lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert\) (times a weight), inertia \((1, 1)\): the result of 9.9.
  3. Eigenvectors depend on units; inertia does not. Level 15 whitens: \(\tilde H = \Sigma^{1/2}H\Sigma^{1/2}\), where \(\Sigma\) is the activation covariance (Level 13). This is exactly 9.10's \(B^\top HB\) with \(B = \Sigma^{1/2}\): measuring each direction in units of its natural spread, as litres versus buckets. theory-v2 calls this “a choice, not a consequence”. Its eigenvectors differ from the raw Hessian's; by Sylvester's law its inertia does not. theory-v2 Remark 2.1 uses exactly this invariance.
  4. “No interaction” axes. 9.8 showed that eigenvectors are the directions with no pairwise interaction. H-space is built from the top eigenvectors of an energy operator made from such Hessians. Asking “which directions carry the most curvature?” is asking for eigenvectors with the largest eigenvalues.
  5. Size. The squared Frobenius norm and the sum of squared eigenvalues are the same number. The energy operators of Level 15 are averages of squared (whitened) Hessians, so they are positive semidefinite: they measure squared curvature, averaged over texts, positions and readouts.

Exercise 9.1. For \(f(x_1, x_2) = x_1^2x_2 + x_2^3\), find the Hessian at \((1, 2)\) and its determinant. Is it a bowl, a dome or a saddle there?

Show answer

First derivatives: \(\partial f/\partial x_1 = 2x_1x_2\), \(\partial f/\partial x_2 = x_1^2 + 3x_2^2\). Second: \(\partial^2 f/\partial x_1^2 = 2x_2 = 4\), \(\partial^2 f/\partial x_1\partial x_2 = 2x_1 = 2\), \(\partial^2 f/\partial x_2^2 = 6x_2 = 12\). \(H = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 12\end{pmatrix}\), \(\det H = 48 - 4 = 44 \gt 0\), \(\operatorname{tr} H = 16 \gt 0\): both eigenvalues positive (they are 3.53 and 12.47), so the local shape is a bowl. (The point is not a critical point, so this is curvature, not a minimum.)

Exercise 9.5. Classify the critical point at the origin of \(f(x_1, x_2) = x_1^2 - 4x_1x_2 + x_2^2\). Then classify the origin for \(x_1^4 + x_2^4\), and explain why the test fails there.

Show answer

Step 1: the Hessian. Differentiate term by term. \(x_1^2\) twice in \(x_1\) gives 2. \(-4x_1x_2\) once in each input gives \(-4\). \(x_2^2\) twice in \(x_2\) gives 2. So \(H = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -4 \\ -4 & 2\end{pmatrix}\) (the same at every point, since \(f\) is quadratic). Step 2: the test. \(\det H = 2\times2 - (-4)\times(-4) = 4 - 16 = -12 \lt 0\): a saddle, even though both diagonal entries are positive. Along \((1, 1)\): \(\mathbf{v}^\top H\mathbf{v} = 2 - 8 + 2 = -4\), down. For \(x_1^4 + x_2^4\): every second derivative at the origin is 0 (for example \(12x_1^2 = 0\)), so \(\det H = 0\) and the test is silent. The origin is in fact a minimum, but you need the fourth-order terms to see it.
