Level 5: Two inputs: partial derivatives and the gradient

When an output depends on two things, you can ask how it changes with each one while the other stays put. Those two answers, side by side, form an arrow that points uphill.

By the end of this level you can
  • read a function of two inputs as a surface and as a contour map;
  • compute a partial derivative by holding the other input fixed, and give its unit;
  • predict the change in output for a small move in both inputs at once;
  • build the gradient, compute the rate of change in any direction, and explain why the gradient points straight uphill, at right angles to the contours;
  • explain why “the steepest direction” depends on the units you measure the inputs in.

Prerequisites: Level 2 (derivatives), Level 3 (second derivatives) and Level 4 (the linear approximation \(f(x_0) + f'(x_0)\,\Delta x\)).

5.1 One output, two inputs

Most things worth predicting depend on more than one thing. A cake depends on oven temperature and baking time. A vote share depends on campaign spending and turnout. A language model's next-word probability depends on 5,120 numbers at each position (Level 14). We start with two inputs, because two is enough to see every new idea, and two inputs still fit on a page.

The farm

A farmer chooses how much water \(W\) (in buckets; one bucket is 10 litres) and how much fertiliser \(F\) (in bags) to use on a field. The yield \(Y\), in bushels, is given by this toy formula:

$$ Y(W, F) = 2W + 3F + 0.5\,WF - 0.25\,W^2 - 0.5\,F^2. $$

It is a made-up formula, chosen to be simple and to behave like a real field. Each piece has a meaning and a unit:

  • \(2W\): each bucket adds 2 bushels, before anything else is counted. The 2 has unit bushels per bucket.
  • \(3F\): each bag adds 3 bushels. The 3 has unit bushels per bag.
  • \(0.5\,WF\): water and fertiliser help each other. The 0.5 has unit bushels per (bucket × bag). This term is the star of Level 6.
  • \(-0.25\,W^2\): too much water drowns the roots. The 0.25 has unit bushels per bucket².
  • \(-0.5\,F^2\): too much fertiliser burns the plants. The 0.5 has unit bushels per bag².

Every term ends up in bushels. For example, \(0.5\ \tfrac{\text{bushels}}{\text{bucket}\cdot\text{bag}} \times 4\ \text{buckets} \times 2\ \text{bags} = 4\ \text{bushels}\).

Worked example: the yield of the plan (4, 2)

Use 4 buckets and 2 bags. Compute each term on its own line:

$$ 2W = 2 \times 4 = 8 $$ $$ 3F = 3 \times 2 = 6 $$ $$ 0.5\,WF = 0.5 \times 4 \times 2 = 4 $$ $$ -0.25\,W^2 = -0.25 \times 16 = -4 $$ $$ -0.5\,F^2 = -0.5 \times 4 = -2 $$ $$ Y(4, 2) = 8 + 6 + 4 - 4 - 2 = 12\ \text{bushels}. $$

5.2 Two pictures: the surface and the contour map

A function of one input has a graph: a curve. A function of two inputs needs one more dimension. Put the plan \((W, F)\) on the floor, and stand a pole at each spot whose height is \(Y(W, F)\). The tops of all the poles form a surface: a hill.

A 3-D picture is hard to read precisely. Hikers solved this long ago with the contour map. Look at the hill from straight above. Draw a line through all the spots at the same height, for several heights. Each line is a contour line (or level curve). Where contours are close together, the hill is steep. Where they are far apart, it is gentle.

For the farm, a contour line is a set of plans that all give the same yield. Economists call such a line an isoquant. A farmer short on fertiliser can slide along a contour, swapping fertiliser for water, without losing yield.

5.3 Partial derivatives: hold everything else fixed

We know how to take a derivative of a function of one input. A function of two inputs has two inputs to wiggle. So ask two separate questions:

  1. If I add a little water and keep the fertiliser exactly as it is, how fast does yield change?
  2. If I add a little fertiliser and keep the water exactly as it is, how fast does yield change?

Each answer is a partial derivative. The first is written \(\partial Y/\partial W\), read “partial Y, partial W”. The curly \(\partial\) is there to remind you that the other input is held fixed. Economists call \(\partial Y/\partial W\) the marginal product of water. Social scientists use the Latin phrase ceteris paribus, “all else equal”, for exactly this idea.

Slices: the one-input functions hiding inside

Holding \(F\) fixed turns \(Y\) into a function of \(W\) alone. Geometrically, you cut the hill with a vertical knife along the line \(F = 2\). The cut edge is a curve: the W-slice. Write it out by putting \(F = 2\) into the formula:

$$ Y(W, 2) = 2W + 3(2) + 0.5\,W(2) - 0.25\,W^2 - 0.5\,(2)^2 $$ $$ = 2W + 6 + W - 0.25\,W^2 - 2 $$ $$ = 3W - 0.25\,W^2 + 4. $$

That is an ordinary one-input function. Its derivative, by the rules of Level 2, is \(3 - 0.5\,W\). At \(W = 4\): \(3 - 2 = 1\) bushel per bucket. So

$$ \frac{\partial Y}{\partial W}(4, 2) = 1\ \text{bushel per bucket}. $$

Now cut the other way, along \(W = 4\). Put \(W = 4\) into the formula:

$$ Y(4, F) = 2(4) + 3F + 0.5(4)F - 0.25\,(4)^2 - 0.5\,F^2 $$ $$ = 8 + 3F + 2F - 4 - 0.5\,F^2 $$ $$ = 5F - 0.5\,F^2 + 4. $$

Its derivative is \(5 - F\). At \(F = 2\): \(5 - 2 = 3\) bushels per bag. So

$$ \frac{\partial Y}{\partial F}(4, 2) = 3\ \text{bushels per bag}. $$
Partial derivative \(\partial f/\partial x\) at a point is the ordinary derivative of the slice through that point along \(x\), with every other input frozen at its value there. Its unit is (output unit) per (unit of \(x\)).

Check with real numbers: a finite difference

Never trust a formula you have not checked. Add 0.01 bucket and keep \(F = 2\):

$$ Y(4.01, 2) = 8.02 + 6 + 4.01 - 4.020025 - 2 = 12.009975. $$ $$ \frac{Y(4.01, 2) - Y(4, 2)}{0.01} = \frac{0.009975}{0.01} = 0.9975\ \text{bushels per bucket} \approx 1. $$

Add 0.01 bag and keep \(W = 4\): \(Y(4, 2.01) = 12.02995\), so the ratio is \(0.02995 / 0.01 = 2.995 \approx 3\) bushels per bag. Both agree. The small gaps (0.0025 and 0.005) are the curvature terms from Level 4, and they shrink if you use a smaller step.

Two patterns, two mechanisms:

  • Along a row (more water, same fertiliser), the value drops by 1 for every 2 buckets. That is 0.5 bushels per bucket for each extra bucket. Each new bucket is worth less than the last: diminishing returns. In derivative language, \(\partial^2 Y/\partial W^2 = -0.5\) bushels per bucket². Likewise \(\partial^2 Y/\partial F^2 = -1\) bushels per bag² (differentiate \(3 + 0.5W - F\) in \(F\)).
  • Down a column (more fertiliser, same water), the value rises by 1 for every 2 bags. That is 0.5 bushels per bucket for each extra bag. Fertiliser makes water more valuable. This cross-effect is not a curvature along either input. It is an interaction, and Level 6 is all about it.

Mechanics in one line: start at \((4, 2)\), where a bucket is worth 1 bushel. Add one bag, keeping water at 4. Now a bucket is worth \(2 + 0.5 \times 3 - 2 = 1.5\) bushels. The bag did not just add yield; it changed the value of water.

5.6 Moving both inputs: the linear approximation

Suppose you change both: \(\Delta W\) more buckets and \(\Delta F\) more bags. How much does the yield change? Do it in two legs.

  1. Leg 1: change water only. The yield changes by about \(\frac{\partial Y}{\partial W}\,\Delta W\) (Level 4, order 1).
  2. Leg 2: from there, change fertiliser only. The yield changes by about \(\frac{\partial Y}{\partial F}\,\Delta F\), using the partial at the end of leg 1. For small moves that is almost the partial at the start.

Add the legs:

First-order change in two inputs $$ \Delta Y \approx \frac{\partial Y}{\partial W}\,\Delta W + \frac{\partial Y}{\partial F}\,\Delta F. $$ Units: (bushels per bucket) × buckets + (bushels per bag) × bags = bushels. Geometrically, this replaces the hill near the point by a flat tilted sheet: the tangent plane. It is the two-input version of the tangent line.

Worked example: from (4, 2) to (5, 3)

Prediction: \(\Delta Y \approx 1 \times 1 + 3 \times 1 = 4\) bushels. Truth: \(Y(5, 3) - Y(4, 2)\). Compute \(Y(5, 3) = 10 + 9 + 7.5 - 6.25 - 4.5 = 15.75\). So the true change is \(15.75 - 12 = 3.75\) bushels. The prediction is 0.25 too high.

Where does the 0.25 go? Because the farm is a quadratic, we can expand exactly. Put \(W + \Delta W\) and \(F + \Delta F\) into the formula, multiply out, and subtract \(Y(W, F)\). Every term that does not contain a \(\Delta\) cancels, and what is left is

$$ \Delta Y = \underbrace{(2 + 0.5F - 0.5W)}_{\partial Y/\partial W}\,\Delta W + \underbrace{(3 + 0.5W - F)}_{\partial Y/\partial F}\,\Delta F \;-\; 0.25\,\Delta W^2 \;-\; 0.5\,\Delta F^2 \;+\; 0.5\,\Delta W\,\Delta F. $$

With \(\Delta W = \Delta F = 1\) at \((4, 2)\):

$$ \Delta Y = 1 + 3 - 0.25 - 0.5 + 0.5 = 3.75. $$

Exact. The three extra pieces are second-order: water's diminishing returns (−0.25), fertiliser's diminishing returns (−0.5) and the interaction (+0.5). For a small move like \(\Delta W = \Delta F = 0.1\), they are 100 times smaller (−0.0025, −0.005, +0.005), and the linear approximation is nearly perfect.

5.7 The gradient: both partials in one arrow

Put the two partial derivatives side by side:

$$ \nabla Y = \left( \frac{\partial Y}{\partial W},\ \frac{\partial Y}{\partial F} \right) = (2 + 0.5F - 0.5W,\ \ 3 + 0.5W - F). $$

This pair is the gradient, written \(\nabla Y\) and read “grad Y”. At \((4, 2)\) it is \((1, 3)\). The first entry is in bushels per bucket, the second in bushels per bag. A pair of numbers like this is a vector (Level 7 treats vectors in general); vectors are written in bold, like \(\mathbf{u}\), from here on.

Draw the gradient as an arrow on the map, starting at the plan: 1 map unit across (water) and 3 map units up (fertiliser). To draw it we had to decide that “1 bushel per bucket” and “1 bushel per bag” are the same length on paper. Section 5.10 shows that this decision matters.

Its length, by Pythagoras, is

$$ \lVert \nabla Y \rVert = \sqrt{1^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{10} \approx 3.162. $$

At the best plan \((14, 10)\): \(\partial Y/\partial W = 2 + 5 - 7 = 0\) and \(\partial Y/\partial F = 3 + 7 - 10 = 0\). The gradient is \((0, 0)\): no arrow. At the top of a hill there is no uphill. Solving “both partials equal zero” is how we find the top: \(2 + 0.5F - 0.5W = 0\) gives \(W = 4 + F\); putting that into \(3 + 0.5W - F = 0\) gives \(3 + 2 + 0.5F - F = 0\), so \(F = 10\) and \(W = 14\). Then \(Y(14, 10) = 28 + 30 + 70 - 49 - 50 = 29\) bushels. (That this flat spot is a top and not a saddle is checked with second derivatives at Level 9.)

5.8 The directional derivative: any direction you like

Partials answer “what if I change only water?” and “what if I change only fertiliser?”. A farmer can also change both in a fixed mix: say 0.6 bucket for every 0.8 bag. How fast does yield change per unit walked in that direction?

Describe the direction by a pair \(\mathbf{u} = (u_W, u_F)\) of length 1 on the map, so \(u_W^2 + u_F^2 = 1\). For example \((0.6, 0.8)\): \(0.36 + 0.64 = 1\). Walk a small distance \(\Delta s\) map units in that direction. The water changes by \(\Delta W = u_W\,\Delta s\) and the fertiliser by \(\Delta F = u_F\,\Delta s\). Put these into the first-order change from section 5.6:

$$ \Delta Y \approx \frac{\partial Y}{\partial W}\,u_W\,\Delta s + \frac{\partial Y}{\partial F}\,u_F\,\Delta s. $$

Divide by \(\Delta s\), and let \(\Delta s\) shrink to zero so the “≈” becomes “=”:

Directional derivative $$ D_{\mathbf{u}} Y = \frac{\partial Y}{\partial W}\,u_W + \frac{\partial Y}{\partial F}\,u_F = \nabla Y \cdot \mathbf{u}. $$ The dot means: multiply matching entries and add. Unit: bushels per map unit. The two partials are the special cases \(\mathbf{u} = (1, 0)\) and \(\mathbf{u} = (0, 1)\).

At \((4, 2)\) with \(\mathbf{u} = (0.6, 0.8)\): \(D_{\mathbf{u}} Y = 1 \times 0.6 + 3 \times 0.8 = 0.6 + 2.4 = 3.0\) bushels per map unit.

Every direction at once

A length-1 arrow at angle \(\theta\) from the water axis has across part \(\cos\theta\) and up part \(\sin\theta\). So \(\mathbf{u} = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\) and, at \((4, 2)\),

$$ D_{\mathbf{u}} Y = 1 \times \cos\theta + 3 \times \sin\theta. $$

The most negative value is in the opposite direction, 251.6°. And at 161.6°, at right angles to the gradient, the rate is zero.

5.9 Steepest ascent, and why the gradient crosses contours at right angles

The table showed it with numbers. Here is why it must be so. Level 7 proves that for any two arrows, \(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = \lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert\cos(\text{angle between them})\). Use it with \(\nabla Y\) and the length-1 direction \(\mathbf{u}\), and call the angle between them \(\alpha\):

$$ D_{\mathbf{u}} Y = \lVert \nabla Y \rVert \times 1 \times \cos\alpha. $$

Now \(\cos\alpha\) is at most 1, reached at \(\alpha = 0°\). So:

  • Steepest ascent. The fastest way uphill is along the gradient (\(\alpha = 0°\)), and the rate there is \(\lVert\nabla Y\rVert\). The fastest way downhill is straight against it (\(\alpha = 180°\)). Walking against the gradient, step after step, is gradient descent: how neural networks are trained (Level 10).
  • Contours. Walking along a contour, the yield does not change, so \(D_{\mathbf{u}}Y = 0\), so \(\cos\alpha = 0\), so \(\alpha = 90°\). The gradient is perpendicular to the contour line through the point.

Mechanics: the contour direction at (4, 2)

Along the contour, to first order, \(1 \times \Delta W + 3 \times \Delta F = 0\). So \(\Delta F = -\Delta W / 3\): one third of a bag less for each extra bucket. A direction along the contour is \((3, -1)\). Check it is perpendicular to the gradient: \((1, 3)\cdot(3, -1) = 3 - 3 = 0\). Now test with real numbers. Take a step of 0.01 times each direction:

  • along the contour, to \((4.03, 1.99)\): \(Y = 11.999575\), a change of only −0.000425 bushels (a second-order leftover);
  • along the gradient, the same distance, to \((4.01, 2.03)\): \(Y = 12.099675\), a change of +0.0997 bushels.

Same distance walked. More than two hundred times more yield change one way than the other.

5.10 “Steepest” depends on your ruler

This is the one non-obvious point of the page. The gradient's two entries have different units: bushels per bucket and bushels per bag. To draw the arrow, or to say which direction is “steepest”, we had to treat 1 bucket and 1 bag as the same length. That is a choice, not a fact about the farm.

Worked example: measure water in litres

Let \(L\) be the water in litres, so \(L = 10\,W\). One more litre is a tenth of a bucket, so it adds a tenth as much yield:

$$ \frac{\partial Y}{\partial L} = \frac{\partial Y}{\partial W} \times \frac{1\ \text{bucket}}{10\ \text{litres}} = 1 \times 0.1 = 0.1\ \text{bushels per litre at } (4, 2). $$

In (litre, bag) units the gradient is \((0.1, 3)\). Its direction is almost pure fertiliser. Compare the steepest-ascent mixes:

  • bucket ruler: gradient \((1, 3)\), so walk 1 bucket for every 3 bags, that is 0.333 buckets per bag;
  • litre ruler: gradient \((0.1, 3)\), so walk 0.1 litre for every 3 bags, that is 0.0333 litres = 0.00333 buckets per bag.

The “steepest” mix of water and fertiliser changed by a factor of 100. The farm did not change. Only the ruler did. (The factor is \(10^2\): one 10 because each litre is worth a tenth as much yield, and another 10 because a step of one litre is a tenth as much water.)

A real-world version: suppose (a toy assumption) water costs 1 dollar a bucket and fertiliser 5 dollars a bag. Per dollar, water gives \(1 / 1 = 1\) bushel and fertiliser gives \(3 / 5 = 0.6\) bushels. Measured in dollars, the best next dollar goes on water. Measured in buckets and bags, the best next step is mostly fertiliser. Both are right. They answer different questions.

The partials are facts; “steepest” is a convention The values \(\partial Y/\partial W = 1\) bushel per bucket and \(\partial Y/\partial F = 3\) bushels per bag do not depend on any choice. The steepest direction, the length of the gradient and the right angle with the contours all depend on how you compare a bucket with a bag. Before you say a direction is “the most important”, say what ruler you used.

5.11 Why this matters for H-space

  1. Directions, not just inputs. In a language model the residual state at one position is 5,120 numbers (Level 14). Interesting things rarely live along one of those numbers. They live along directions: fixed mixes of all 5,120, exactly like the water-and-fertiliser mix \(\mathbf{u}\). The rate of change along such a direction is a directional derivative, \(\nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u}\).
  2. First order is the J-lens. Stacking gradients for many outputs gives the Jacobian (Level 8). The J-lens of Level 14 is built from these first derivatives. H-space asks the same questions one derivative higher: how does the gradient itself change when you move? Section 5.5 is the first glimpse of that.
  3. The ruler problem is real there. Different directions in the residual stream naturally vary by very different amounts. Comparing raw rates along them is like comparing per-litre with per-bag. Level 15 measures every direction in units of its own natural spread (one standard deviation, “1σ”), a step called whitening. Section 5.10 is the reason it is needed.

Exercise 5.8. At \((6, 4)\) you add one small extra bucket. To first order, how many bags must you remove to keep the yield the same?

Show answer

Stay on the contour: \(1 \times \Delta W + 2 \times \Delta F = 0\). With \(\Delta W = 1\) (per bucket), \(\Delta F = -0.5\). Remove 0.5 bag per bucket. The step \((1, -0.5)\) is perpendicular to the gradient \((1, 2)\): \(1 - 1 = 0\).

Exercise 5.9. At \((4, 2)\), what is the rate of change of yield per extra litre of water?

A litre is 0.1 bucket, so \(\partial Y/\partial L = 1\ \tfrac{\text{bushel}}{\text{bucket}} \times 0.1\ \tfrac{\text{bucket}}{\text{litre}} = 0.1\) bushel per litre.
