Level 12: The second-order chain rule and the rectangle identity

Where a network's curvature is created, how it is carried back to the layer you study, and what a finite interaction really measures.

By the end of this level you can
  • take the second derivative of a function of a function, and name its two sources of curvature, with units;
  • write the vector second-order chain rule and use it on the tiny network;
  • state the second-order adjoint (theory-v2 Lemma 1), prove it in one dimension, and run it by hand;
  • say where curvature is created inside a network: threshold sites and product sites (Lemma 2);
  • prove that a four-corner interaction equals the Hessian averaged over a parallelogram (Lemma 3), and compute both sides;
  • explain why the Hessian at one corner can badly mispredict an interaction at natural move sizes, and read the 0.8B check in theory-v2 §3.

Prerequisites: Level 4 (Taylor, third derivative), Level 6 (the four-corner interaction), Level 9 (the Hessian, eigenvalues, inertia), Level 10 (chain rule, product rule, adjoints, the tiny network) and Level 11 (Hessian-vector products).

12.1 Two sources of curvature: the second-order chain rule in one dimension

Level 10 gave the first derivative of a chain. Now differentiate once more. In this section \(f\) is the outer machine and \(g\) the inner one, so the chain is \(f(g(x))\).

The second-order chain rule $$ \big(f(g(x))\big)'' = f''\big(g(x)\big)\,g'(x)^2 \;+\; f'\big(g(x)\big)\,g''(x). $$ Two sources of curvature: the outer curvature, seen through the inner stretch squared; plus the inner curvature, weighted by the outer slope.

Derivation, every step

  1. First derivative (chain rule): \(\big(f(g(x))\big)' = f'(g(x))\cdot g'(x)\). This is a product of two functions of \(x\).
  2. Product rule (Level 10): differentiate the first factor and keep the second, then keep the first and differentiate the second: $$ \big(f(g(x))\big)'' = \big[f'(g(x))\big]'\cdot g'(x) + f'(g(x))\cdot g''(x). $$
  3. The bracket is again a chain: outer \(f'\), inner \(g\). So \(\big[f'(g(x))\big]' = f''(g(x))\cdot g'(x)\).
  4. Substitute: \(f''(g(x))\,g'(x)\cdot g'(x) + f'(g(x))\,g''(x)\). The two \(g'\) factors give \(g'(x)^2\). Done.

Intuition pump: a photocopier and a warped sheet

  • Stretch only. Draw the parabola \(y = u^2\) and enlarge the horizontal axis on a photocopier by a factor 2, so \(u = 2x\). Then \(y = 4x^2\) and \(y'' = 8\). The original curvature was \(f'' = 2\); it became \(2\times2^2\). Curvature is “per input unit, per input unit”, so a stretch of the input enters twice. That is the \(g'^2\).
  • Bend only. Take a straight outer machine, \(y = 3u\) (no curvature: \(f'' = 0\)), and feed it a bent inner machine, \(u = x^2\). The output \(y = 3x^2\) is curved anyway: \(y'' = 6 = 3\times2\), outer slope times inner curvature. That is the \(f'g''\) term.

In a network both happen at once, at every layer. This is the whole of Lemma 1 in miniature.

Worked example: an accelerating balloon, every unit

Let the radius grow faster and faster: \(R(\tau) = 2 + 0.25\,\tau^2\) cm, at time \(\tau\) in seconds. The volume is \(V(R) = \tfrac43\pi R^3\) cm³. Take \(\tau = 2\) s.

Step 1: the inner value. Put \(\tau = 2\) into \(R(\tau)\): \(R = 2 + 0.25\times2^2 = 2 + 0.25\times4 = 3\) cm.

Step 2: the inner slope. Differentiate \(R(\tau) = 2 + 0.25\,\tau^2\). The constant 2 gives 0. The power rule gives \(0.25\times2\tau = 0.5\,\tau\). So \(R' = 0.5\,\tau\), and at \(\tau = 2\) s, \(R' = 1\) cm/s.

Step 3: the inner curvature. Differentiate \(R' = 0.5\,\tau\) once more: \(R'' = 0.5\) cm/s², the same at every time.

Step 4: the outer slope. Differentiate \(V(R) = \tfrac43\pi R^3\) by the power rule: \(V'(R) = \tfrac43\pi\times3R^2 = 4\pi R^2\). At \(R = 3\) cm: \(4\pi\times9 = 36\pi = 113.10\) cm³/cm.

Step 5: the outer curvature. Differentiate \(4\pi R^2\): \(V''(R) = 8\pi R\). At \(R = 3\) cm: \(24\pi = 75.40\) cm³/cm².

Step 6: the first term. \(V''\cdot R'^2 = 75.40\ \tfrac{\text{cm}^3}{\text{cm}^2}\times\big(1\ \tfrac{\text{cm}}{\text{s}}\big)^2 = 75.40\ \tfrac{\text{cm}^3}{\text{s}^2}\). The cm² on the bottom cancels the cm² from squaring the rate.

Step 7: the second term. \(V'\cdot R'' = 113.10\ \tfrac{\text{cm}^3}{\text{cm}}\times0.5\ \tfrac{\text{cm}}{\text{s}^2} = 56.55\ \tfrac{\text{cm}^3}{\text{s}^2}\).

Step 8: add. \(\dfrac{d^2V}{d\tau^2} = 75.40 + 56.55 = 131.95\) cm³/s². In exact form: \(24\pi + 18\pi = 42\pi\).

Mechanics check. The second difference \(\big(V(\tau{+}0.001) - 2V(\tau) + V(\tau{-}0.001)\big)/0.001^2\) at \(\tau = 2\) s is 131.95 cm³/s². Both units agree: a rate of change of a filling rate, “cm³ per second, per second”.

The common mistake Writing \(f''(g(x))\cdot g''(x)\), by analogy with the first-order rule. It is wrong in units (cm³/cm² × cm/s² is not cm³/s²) and wrong in value. Units catch it immediately.

12.2 Many inputs: the vector second-order chain rule

Now let an inner function \(\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{x})\) have several outputs \(p_1, p_2, \ldots\), and an outer function \(G\) turn them into one number. Let \(J\) be the Jacobian of \(\mathbf{p}\) at \(\mathbf{x}\).

Vector form $$ \nabla^2(G\circ\mathbf{p})(\mathbf{x}) = J^\top\,\nabla^2G\big(\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{x})\big)\,J \;+\; \sum_i \frac{\partial G}{\partial p_i}\big(\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{x})\big)\,\nabla^2 p_i(\mathbf{x}). $$ The same two sources: the outer curvature, transported by the inner Jacobian on both sides; plus each inner output's curvature, weighted by how much the outer function cares about that output.

Derivation, entry by entry

  1. First derivative (chain rule with paths, Level 10): \(\dfrac{\partial (G\circ\mathbf{p})}{\partial x_j} = \sum_i \dfrac{\partial G}{\partial p_i}\,\dfrac{\partial p_i}{\partial x_j}\).
  2. Differentiate with respect to \(x_k\). Each term is a product, so use the product rule: $$ \frac{\partial^2 (G\circ\mathbf{p})}{\partial x_j\,\partial x_k} = \sum_i \Big[\frac{\partial}{\partial x_k}\frac{\partial G}{\partial p_i}\Big]\frac{\partial p_i}{\partial x_j} + \sum_i \frac{\partial G}{\partial p_i}\,\frac{\partial^2 p_i}{\partial x_j\,\partial x_k}. $$
  3. The bracket is a chain again: \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_k}\frac{\partial G}{\partial p_i} = \sum_l \frac{\partial^2 G}{\partial p_i\,\partial p_l}\frac{\partial p_l}{\partial x_k}\).
  4. Substitute step 3 into the first sum of step 2. Rename the slopes as Jacobian entries, \(\partial p_i/\partial x_j = J_{ij}\) and \(\partial p_l/\partial x_k = J_{lk}\) (row = output, column = input), and the outer second derivatives as \((\nabla^2G)_{il}\). The first sum becomes \(\sum_{i,l} J_{ij}\,(\nabla^2G)_{il}\,J_{lk}\).
  5. Use the transpose to put the indices in matrix-product order: \(J_{ij} = (J^\top)_{ji}\). So the first sum is \(\sum_{i,l} (J^\top)_{ji}\,(\nabla^2G)_{il}\,J_{lk}\). A product of three matrices has entry \((j,k)\) equal to exactly this double sum (row \(j\) of the first, through the middle, to column \(k\) of the last). So the first sum is entry \((j,k)\) of \(J^\top\nabla^2G\,J\).
  6. The second sum of step 2 is, entry by entry, \(\sum_i \frac{\partial G}{\partial p_i}(\nabla^2p_i)_{jk}\): entry \((j,k)\) of the matrix \(\sum_i \frac{\partial G}{\partial p_i}\nabla^2p_i\). Done.

Toy check of the transport term. Let \(\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{x}) = (2x_1,\ x_2)\) and \(G(\mathbf{p}) = p_1p_2\). Then \(J = \operatorname{diag}(2, 1)\), \(\nabla^2G = \begin{pmatrix}0 & 1\\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}\), and every \(\nabla^2p_i = 0\). The rule gives \(J^\top\nabla^2G\,J = \begin{pmatrix}0 & 2\\ 2 & 0\end{pmatrix}\). Directly: \(G(\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{x})) = 2x_1x_2\), whose mixed partial is 2. Same.

Units check: (output per \(p_i\) per \(p_l\)) × (\(p_i\) per \(x_j\)) × (\(p_l\) per \(x_k\)) = output per \(x_j\) per \(x_k\), the unit of a Hessian entry.

Worked example: the tiny network's Hessian, a third way

Take \(p_i = \operatorname{silu}(z_i)\) with \(z_1 = x_1 - x_2 + 0.5\) and \(z_2 = 0.5x_1 + x_2 - 1\), and \(G(\mathbf{p}) = c_1p_1 + c_2p_2\) with \(\mathbf{c} = (1.5, -1)\).

Step 1: the outer curvature. \(G\) is linear, so all its second derivatives are 0: \(\nabla^2G = 0\), and the first term of the rule vanishes.

Step 2: the outer slopes. \(\partial G/\partial p_i = c_i\).

Step 3: first derivative of \(p_i\). \(z_i = W_{i1}x_1 + W_{i2}x_2 + \text{a fixed bias}\) (for example \(z_1 = x_1 - x_2 + 0.5\)), so \(\partial z_i/\partial x_j = W_{ij}\). By the chain rule, \(\partial p_i/\partial x_j = \operatorname{silu}'(z_i)\,W_{ij}\).

Step 4: second derivative of \(p_i\). Differentiate step 3 with respect to \(x_k\). \(W_{ij}\) is a fixed number. \(\operatorname{silu}'(z_i)\) changes at rate \(\operatorname{silu}''(z_i)\,W_{ik}\) (chain rule again). So \(\partial^2p_i/\partial x_j\partial x_k = \operatorname{silu}''(z_i)\,W_{ij}W_{ik}\).

Step 5: matrix form. The numbers \(W_{ij}W_{ik}\), over all \(j, k\), are the entries of the outer product \(\mathbf{w}_i\mathbf{w}_i^\top\), where \(\mathbf{w}_1 = (1, -1)\) and \(\mathbf{w}_2 = (0.5, 1)\) are the rows of \(W\). So \(\nabla^2p_i = \operatorname{silu}''(z_i)\,\mathbf{w}_i\mathbf{w}_i^\top\), and the rule gives \(H = \sum_i c_i\operatorname{silu}''(z_i)\,\mathbf{w}_i\mathbf{w}_i^\top\).

Step 6: the two outer products.

$$ \mathbf{w}_1\mathbf{w}_1^\top = \begin{pmatrix}1\cdot1 & 1\cdot(-1)\\ (-1)\cdot1 & (-1)(-1)\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1 & -1\\ -1 & 1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{w}_2\mathbf{w}_2^\top = \begin{pmatrix}0.5\cdot0.5 & 0.5\cdot1\\ 1\cdot0.5 & 1\cdot1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0.25 & 0.5\\ 0.5 & 1\end{pmatrix}. $$

Step 7: the weights. At \(\mathbf{x} = (1,2)\): \(c_1\operatorname{silu}''(z_1) = 1.5\times0.4412 = 0.6618\) and \(c_2\operatorname{silu}''(z_2) = -1\times0.1562 = -0.1562\).

Step 8: scale and add. Entry (1,1): \(0.6618\times1 - 0.1562\times0.25 = 0.6618 - 0.0390 = 0.6228\). Entry (1,2): \(0.6618\times(-1) - 0.1562\times0.5 = -0.6618 - 0.0781 = -0.7399\). Entry (2,2): \(0.6618\times1 - 0.1562\times1 = 0.5056\). So

$$ H = 0.6618\begin{pmatrix}1 & -1\\ -1 & 1\end{pmatrix} - 0.1562\begin{pmatrix}0.25 & 0.5\\ 0.5 & 1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0.6228 & -0.7399\\ -0.7399 & 0.5056\end{pmatrix}, $$

the same matrix Level 11 got by double backward. Each piece is rank 1: neuron 1 adds upward curvature along \(\mathbf{w}_1\), neuron 2 adds downward curvature along \(\mathbf{w}_2\).

Worked example: when the outer function curves too

Train against a target \(y^\ast = 0\) with \(\mathcal{L} = \tfrac12(y - y^\ast)^2\). At \(\mathbf{x} = (1, 2)\) the network gives \(y = -1.5095\).

Step 1: the outer function and its derivatives. The outer function is \(G(y) = \tfrac12(y-y^\ast)^2\), a function of one number. By the chain rule, \(G' = y - y^\ast = -1.5095 - 0 = -1.5095\). Differentiating again, \(G'' = 1\).

Step 2: the inner Jacobian. The inner function is the network, with one output and two inputs. Its Jacobian is one row: the gradient laid flat, \(J = \nabla y^\top = (-0.1306, -1.4314)\).

Step 3: substitute into the rule. \(J^\top\,\nabla^2G\,J = \nabla y\cdot1\cdot\nabla y^\top = \nabla y\,\nabla y^\top\) (a column times a row). The second term has one inner output, so the sum has one term: \(G'\,\nabla^2y = (y - y^\ast)\,H\). So

$$ \nabla^2\mathcal{L} = \nabla y\,\nabla y^\top + (y - y^\ast)\,H . $$

Step 4: the outer product. Entry \((j,k)\) of \(\nabla y\,\nabla y^\top\) is \((\partial y/\partial x_j)(\partial y/\partial x_k)\): \((-0.1306)^2 = 0.0171\), \((-0.1306)(-1.4314) = 0.1869\), \((-1.4314)^2 = 2.0488\).

Step 5: scale \(H\). Multiply each entry of \(H\) by \(-1.5095\): \(-1.5095\times0.6228 = -0.9401\), \(-1.5095\times(-0.7399) = 1.1170\), \(-1.5095\times0.5056 = -0.7633\).

Step 6: add entry by entry. \(0.0171 - 0.9401 = -0.9231\) (to four digits from unrounded inputs), \(0.1869 + 1.1170 = 1.3039\), \(2.0488 - 0.7633 = 1.2855\):

$$ \nabla^2\mathcal{L} = \begin{pmatrix}0.0171 & 0.1869\\ 0.1869 & 2.0488\end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix}-0.9401 & 1.1170\\ 1.1170 & -0.7633\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}-0.9231 & 1.3039\\ 1.3039 & 1.2855\end{pmatrix}. $$

The first piece is the Gauss–Newton term (outer curvature carried through the Jacobian). It is never negative along any direction: \(\mathbf{v}^\top\nabla y\,\nabla y^\top\mathbf{v} = (\nabla y\cdot\mathbf{v})^2 \ge 0\) for any direction \(\mathbf{v}\). The second is the network's own curvature, weighted by how far off the output is.

Step 7: compare sizes. Frobenius norm (Level 9) of the first piece: \(\sqrt{0.0171^2 + 2\times0.1869^2 + 2.0488^2} = \sqrt{4.2677} = 2.07\). Of the second: \(\sqrt{0.9401^2 + 2\times1.1170^2 + 0.7633^2} = \sqrt{3.9616} = 1.99\). About the same size.

Step 8: eigenvalues (from trace and determinant, Level 9). Gauss–Newton piece: trace \(0.0171 + 2.0488 = 2.0659\), determinant \(0.0171\times2.0488 - 0.1869^2 = 0.0350 - 0.0349 \approx 0\); it is exactly 0, because a column times a row has rank 1, so the eigenvalues are 2.07 and 0. Sum: trace \(-0.9231 + 1.2855 = 0.3624\), determinant \(-0.9231\times1.2855 - 1.3039^2 = -1.1866 - 1.7002 = -2.8868\), so the eigenvalues are \(\big(0.3624 \pm \sqrt{0.3624^2 + 4\times2.8868}\big)/2 = (0.3624 \pm 3.4173)/2\): 1.89 and −1.53. One up, one down: a saddle. The second piece decides the shape.

12.3 Residual sublayers, and why the identity adds no curvature

A transformer (Level 14) is a chain of residual sublayers. Each one keeps its input and adds a correction computed from it:

$$ \mathbf{y}_{k+1} = \mathbf{y}_k + \varphi_k(\mathbf{y}_k). $$

Real-world example. A shared notebook passed round a team. Each person reads the page and writes an addition; nobody erases. The page after person \(k\) is the page before, plus their note.

The Jacobian of one sublayer is \(A_k = \mathrm{Id} + D\varphi_k\): the identity (the copy) plus the branch's Jacobian. Now its curvature. Component \(i\) of the output is \(y_i + \varphi_{k,i}(\mathbf{y})\). Its Hessian is \(\nabla^2 y_i + \nabla^2\varphi_{k,i}\). The first term is zero: \(y_i\) is linear in \(\mathbf{y}\), and a linear function has no curvature. So:

The skip connection is a straight pipe The identity path copies the state forward and contributes to every Jacobian, but it creates no curvature. All curvature at sublayer \(k\) is created by the branch \(\varphi_k\).

12.4 The second-order adjoint (theory-v2 Lemma 1)

Put 12.2 and 12.3 together over many sublayers. theory-v2 §1 states this as Lemma 1 and labels it standard: the discrete second-order adjoint, or Hessian backpropagation (it cites Christianson 1992, Pearlmutter 1994 and Griewank & Walther 2008, among others). It claims no novelty.

One dimension first, with every step

Take \(K\) sublayers with one number of state, \(y_{k+1} = y_k + \varphi_k(y_k)\), starting from \(y_0 = h\). The objective is the last state, \(F = y_K\) (a linear readout, so it has no curvature of its own). Define:

  • \(A_k = 1 + \varphi_k'(y_k)\): sublayer \(k\)'s slope.
  • \(J_k = A_{k-1}\cdots A_0\), with \(J_0 = 1\): how much \(y_k\) moves per unit move of \(h\) (chain rule).
  • Adjoints, run backwards: \(g_K = 1\), \(g_k = A_k\,g_{k+1}\). So \(g_k = \partial F/\partial y_k\).
  • \(C_k = g_{k+1}\,\varphi_k''(y_k)\): curvature created at sublayer \(k\), measured in units of the objective.

Let \(G_k(y)\) be the objective as a function of the state after \(k\) sublayers. Then \(G_k(y) = G_{k+1}\big(y + \varphi_k(y)\big)\): a chain with outer \(G_{k+1}\) and inner \(y + \varphi_k(y)\). Apply the 1-D second-order chain rule of 12.1. The inner slope is \(A_k\), the inner curvature is \(\varphi_k''\) (the \(y\) part adds none), and the outer slope is \(G_{k+1}' = g_{k+1}\):

$$ G_k'' = G_{k+1}''\,A_k^2 + g_{k+1}\,\varphi_k'' = A_k^2\,G_{k+1}'' + C_k . $$

Now unroll this from the end, one sublayer at a time.

Step 1: the end. \(G_K(y) = y\), because \(F = y_K\). A straight line has no curvature: \(G_K'' = 0\).

Step 2: one sublayer back. Put \(k = K-1\) into the recursion and use Step 1: \(G_{K-1}'' = A_{K-1}^2\times0 + C_{K-1} = C_{K-1}\).

Step 3: two sublayers back. Put \(k = K-2\) and use Step 2: \(G_{K-2}'' = A_{K-2}^2\,C_{K-1} + C_{K-2}\).

Step 4: three sublayers back. Put \(k = K-3\) and use Step 3, then multiply out the bracket:

$$ G_{K-3}'' = A_{K-3}^2\big(A_{K-2}^2C_{K-1} + C_{K-2}\big) + C_{K-3} = (A_{K-2}A_{K-3})^2\,C_{K-1} + A_{K-3}^2\,C_{K-2} + C_{K-3} . $$

Step 5: read the pattern. Each \(C_k\) picks up one factor \(A^2\) for every sublayer between it and the point where we stand. When we reach the start, the factor in front of \(C_k\) is \((A_{k-1}\cdots A_0)^2 = J_k^2\). (For \(k = 0\) there is nothing in between, and \(J_0 = 1\).) So

$$ F''(h) = G_0'' = \sum_{k=0}^{K-1} J_k^2\,C_k . $$

Toy check with \(K = 2\): Step 3 with \(K = 2\) reads \(G_0'' = A_0^2\,C_1 + C_0 = J_1^2C_1 + J_0^2C_0\). The worked example below puts numbers in.

Worked example by hand

Two sublayers, \(\varphi_0(y) = 0.5\,y^2\) and \(\varphi_1(y) = 0.25\,y^2\) (so \(\kappa_0 = 0.5\), \(\kappa_1 = 0.25\)), starting at \(h = y_0 = 1\).

  1. Forward: \(y_1 = 1 + 0.5(1)^2 = 1.5\); \(y_2 = 1.5 + 0.25(1.5)^2 = 1.5 + 0.5625 = 2.0625\). So \(F = 2.0625\).
  2. Slopes: \(\varphi_0' = 2\kappa_0y = y\), so \(A_0 = 1 + 1 = 2\). \(\varphi_1' = 2\kappa_1y = 0.5y\), so \(A_1 = 1 + 0.75 = 1.75\).
  3. Adjoints (backward): \(g_2 = 1\); \(g_1 = A_1g_2 = 1.75\); \(g_0 = A_0g_1 = 3.5\). So \(F'(h) = 3.5\).
  4. Curvature created: \(\varphi_0'' = 2\kappa_0 = 1\), so \(C_0 = g_1\times1 = 1.75\). \(\varphi_1'' = 2\kappa_1 = 0.5\), so \(C_1 = g_2\times0.5 = 0.5\).
  5. Transports: \(J_0 = 1\); \(J_1 = A_0 = 2\).
  6. Sum: \(F''(h) = J_0^2C_0 + J_1^2C_1 = 1\times1.75 + 4\times0.5 = 1.75 + 2 = 3.75\).

Check directly.

Step 1: write \(F\) as one formula. After sublayer 0 the state is \(q = y + 0.5y^2\) (\(q\) is just a short name here). Sublayer 1 adds \(0.25q^2\). So \(F(y) = q + 0.25q^2 = y + 0.5y^2 + 0.25(y + 0.5y^2)^2\).

Step 2: slope of \(q\). \(q' = 1 + y\) (power rule on \(0.5y^2\)). At \(y = 1\): \(q = 1.5\) and \(q' = 2\).

Step 3: first derivative of \(F\). \(F = q + 0.25q^2\). Chain rule on \(0.25q^2\): its derivative is \(0.25\times2q\times q' = 0.5\,q\,q'\). So \(F' = q' + 0.5\,q\,q' = 1 + y + 0.5(y + 0.5y^2)(1 + y)\). At \(y = 1\): \(2 + 0.5\times1.5\times2 = 2 + 1.5 = 3.5\). It matches \(g_0\).

Step 4: second derivative of \(F\). Differentiate \(q'\) to get \(q'' = 1\). The term \(0.5\,q\,q'\) is a product, so the product rule gives \(0.5(q'\cdot q' + q\cdot q'') = 0.5\big((1+y)^2 + (y + 0.5y^2)\big)\). So \(F'' = 1 + 0.5\big[(1+y)^2 + (y + 0.5y^2)\big]\).

Step 5: put in \(y = 1\). \((1+1)^2 = 4\) and \(1 + 0.5 = 1.5\), so \(F'' = 1 + 0.5\times(4 + 1.5) = 1 + 2.75 = 3.75\). Same as Lemma 1.

Reading the numbers. Sublayer 1 creates less curvature (\(C_1 = 0.5\)) than sublayer 0 (\(C_0 = 1.75\)), yet contributes more (2 against 1.75). It sits after sublayer 0, so a nudge at the start reaches it doubled (\(J_1 = 2\)), and curvature is per unit², so the doubling counts twice. Meanwhile sublayer 0's curvature is weighted by \(g_1 = 1.75\): how much the rest of the chain amplifies its output.

The statement in theory-v2 (vector form)

With vectors the squares become transposes on both sides (12.2). In this page's letters (theory-v2 uses \(s, S, I\) for \(k, K, \mathrm{Id}\)): sublayers \(\mathbf{y}_{k+1} = \mathbf{y}_k + \varphi_k(\mathbf{y}_k)\) from \(\mathbf{y}_0 = \mathbf{h}\); \(A_k = \mathrm{Id} + D\varphi_k(\mathbf{y}_k)\); \(J_k = A_{k-1}\cdots A_0\); \(\mathbf{g}_K = \partial F/\partial\mathbf{y}_K\), \(\mathbf{g}_k = A_k^\top\mathbf{g}_{k+1}\); \(C_k = \nabla^2_{\mathbf{y}}\langle\mathbf{g}_{k+1}, \varphi_k(\mathbf{y})\rangle\) at \(\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{y}_k\), with \(\mathbf{g}_{k+1}\) held at its value on the actual run. Then, for every input,

$$ \nabla^2_{\mathbf{h}}F = \sum_{k=0}^{K-1} J_k^\top\,C_k\,J_k . $$

The proof is the 1-D one with the vector rule of 12.2, in four steps.

Step 1: one sublayer as a chain. \(G_k(\mathbf{y}) = G_{k+1}\big(\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{y})\big)\) with inner function \(\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{y}) = \mathbf{y} + \varphi_k(\mathbf{y})\). Its Jacobian is \(J = A_k\).

Step 2: apply the vector rule. \(\nabla^2G_k = A_k^\top\,\nabla^2G_{k+1}\,A_k + \sum_i \frac{\partial G_{k+1}}{\partial y_i}\,\nabla^2p_i\).

Step 3: simplify the second term. Output \(i\) of the inner function is \(y_i + \varphi_{k,i}(\mathbf{y})\). The \(y_i\) part is linear, so \(\nabla^2p_i = \nabla^2\varphi_{k,i}\) (12.3). The outer slopes \(\partial G_{k+1}/\partial y_i\) are the entries of \(\mathbf{g}_{k+1}\), fixed numbers on this run. A fixed weight can move inside a second derivative, so \(\sum_i g_{k+1,i}\nabla^2\varphi_{k,i} = \nabla^2\langle\mathbf{g}_{k+1},\varphi_k\rangle = C_k\). Hence

$$ \nabla^2G_k = A_k^\top\,\nabla^2G_{k+1}\,A_k + C_k . $$

Step 4: unroll from \(\nabla^2G_K = 0\) (\(F\) is linear in \(\mathbf{y}_K\)), exactly as in 1-D. Each \(C_k\) collects \(A_j^\top\) on its left and \(A_j\) on its right for every sublayer \(j\) before it. On the left they stack up as \(A_0^\top A_1^\top\cdots A_{k-1}^\top\), on the right as \(A_{k-1}\cdots A_1A_0 = J_k\). The transpose of a product is the product of the transposes in reverse order (for example \((A_1A_0)^\top = A_0^\top A_1^\top\)), so the left stack is \(J_k^\top\). That gives \(\nabla^2_{\mathbf{h}}F = \sum_k J_k^\top C_kJ_k\).

As an HVP. theory-v2 also gives the forward-over-reverse form (Level 11) for a direction \(\mathbf{v}\): forward \(\dot{\mathbf{y}}_0 = \mathbf{v}\), \(\dot{\mathbf{y}}_{k+1} = A_k\dot{\mathbf{y}}_k\); backward \(\dot{\mathbf{g}}_K = \mathbf{0}\), \(\dot{\mathbf{g}}_k = A_k^\top\dot{\mathbf{g}}_{k+1} + C_k\dot{\mathbf{y}}_k\); output \(\nabla^2F\,\mathbf{v} = \dot{\mathbf{g}}_0\). In the 1-D example with \(v = 1\): \(\dot{y}_0 = 1\), \(\dot{y}_1 = A_0 = 2\); \(\dot{g}_2 = 0\), \(\dot{g}_1 = 1.75\times0 + C_1\dot{y}_1 = 0.5\times2 = 1\), \(\dot{g}_0 = A_0\dot{g}_1 + C_0\dot{y}_0 = 2 + 1.75 = 3.75\). Same 3.75.

If the readout passes through the final RMSNorm (a true logit, Level 14), theory-v2 adds one more term for that norm's own curvature. The H-space objective \(F_c\) is linear in the last state, so it does not need it.

What Lemma 1 does and does not say (theory-v2 §1, Scope)
  • It is exact for each input separately.
  • Averaging over contexts and positions only works term by term, as a sum of averages. It is never “average \(J\)” times “average \(C\)” times “average \(J\)”. Formulas built that way (theory-v2 §§4 to 6) are approximations, not consequences of the lemma.
  • The split into “curvature created at sublayer \(k\)” depends on how you cut the model into sublayers. The total does not.

Implementation check. theory-v2 §1 checked the code, not the mathematics: on Qwen3.5-0.8B, the sum of per-sublayer HVP pieces (hs_anatomy.py) against one full double-backward HVP, largest relative error over 24 batches: \(8.8\times10^{-7}\), \(1.1\times10^{-6}\), \(1.2\times10^{-6}\), \(1.3\times10^{-6}\) and \(1.45\times10^{-6}\) at layers 20, 16, 12, 8 and 4 (results/anatomy_08b.json). For scale: fp32 keeps about 7 significant digits (Level 11), and a missing or wrong term would show up as an error near 1.

12.5 Where curvature is created: threshold sites and product sites (Lemma 2)

Zoom into one sublayer and draw it as a computational graph (Level 10). theory-v2 §2 Lemma 2 (standard second-order reverse-mode differentiation) says every node is one of three kinds, and only two of them create curvature:

  • Linear nodes (weighted sums): no curvature.
  • One-input nodes \(\theta_n\) (SiLU, sigmoid, exp, …), called threshold sites: each adds \(\bar{g}_n\,\theta_n''(z_n)\,\mathbf{a}_n\mathbf{a}_n^\top\).
  • Products of two inputs, called product sites: each adds \(\bar{g}_n\,\operatorname{sym}(\mathbf{a}_n\mathbf{b}_n^\top) = \bar{g}_n(\mathbf{a}_n\mathbf{b}_n^\top + \mathbf{b}_n\mathbf{a}_n^\top)\).

Here \(\bar{g}_n\) is the adjoint at the node's output (how much the objective cares about it), and \(\mathbf{a}_n\), \(\mathbf{b}_n\) are the reads: the gradients of the node's inputs with respect to the sublayer input. Exactly:

$$ \nabla^2_{\mathbf{y}}\big[\mathbf{g}\cdot\varphi(\mathbf{y})\big] = \sum_{\text{one-input } n}\bar{g}_n\,\theta_n''(z_n)\,\mathbf{a}_n\mathbf{a}_n^\top + \sum_{\text{product } n}\bar{g}_n\,\operatorname{sym}(\mathbf{a}_n\mathbf{b}_n^\top). $$

Why these forms. Near the current point, treat each node's input as a straight read of \(\mathbf{y}\) (any curvature of the input itself belongs to the nodes upstream and is counted there).

Step 1: a one-input node. It computes \(\theta_n(z_n)\) with \(z_n = \mathbf{a}_n\cdot\mathbf{y} + \text{const}\), so \(\partial z_n/\partial y_j = a_{n,j}\) (entry \(j\) of \(\mathbf{a}_n\)).

Step 2: its first derivative (chain rule): \(\partial\theta_n/\partial y_j = \theta_n'(z_n)\,a_{n,j}\).

Step 3: its second derivative (chain rule again; \(a_{n,j}\) is fixed): \(\partial^2\theta_n/\partial y_j\partial y_k = \theta_n''(z_n)\,a_{n,j}a_{n,k}\). Over all \(j, k\) that is the matrix \(\theta_n''\,\mathbf{a}_n\mathbf{a}_n^\top\).

Step 4: a product node. It computes \(z_n \zeta_n\) with \(z_n = \mathbf{a}_n\cdot\mathbf{y} + \text{const}\) and \(\zeta_n = \mathbf{b}_n\cdot\mathbf{y} + \text{const}\). By the product rule, \(\partial(z_n\zeta_n)/\partial y_j = a_{n,j}\,\zeta_n + z_n\,b_{n,j}\).

Step 5: differentiate with respect to \(y_k\). \(a_{n,j}\) and \(b_{n,j}\) are fixed; \(\zeta_n\) changes at rate \(b_{n,k}\) and \(z_n\) at rate \(a_{n,k}\). So \(\partial^2(z_n\zeta_n)/\partial y_j\partial y_k = a_{n,j}b_{n,k} + b_{n,j}a_{n,k}\). Over all \(j, k\) that is \(\mathbf{a}_n\mathbf{b}_n^\top + \mathbf{b}_n\mathbf{a}_n^\top\).

Step 6: weight by the adjoint. The objective sees each node through \(\bar g_n\), a fixed number on this run, which multiplies the node's Hessian. That gives the two terms of the formula.

The tiny network is two threshold sites

The network's nodes are: a linear map (\(W\)), two SiLUs, and a linear readout (\(\mathbf{c}\)). The SiLUs are the only threshold sites. For SiLU \(i\): the adjoint at its output is \(c_i\), its read is \(\mathbf{w}_i\). So \(H = \sum_i c_i\operatorname{silu}''(z_i)\,\mathbf{w}_i\mathbf{w}_i^\top\): exactly the two rank-1 pieces of 12.2.

A product site, with numbers

Let the reads be \(\mathbf{a}_n = (1, 0)\) and \(\mathbf{b}_n = (1, 1)\), with adjoint \(\bar{g}_n = 1\).

Step 1: write out the site. \(\mathbf{a}_n\cdot\mathbf{x} = x_1\) and \(\mathbf{b}_n\cdot\mathbf{x} = x_1 + x_2\). So the site computes \(x_1(x_1 + x_2) = x_1^2 + x_1x_2\).

Step 2: its Hessian, directly. \(\partial^2/\partial x_1^2\) of \(x_1^2\) is 2. \(\partial^2/\partial x_1\partial x_2\) of \(x_1x_2\) is 1. Nothing has \(x_2^2\), so the (2,2) entry is 0. The Hessian is \(\begin{pmatrix}2 & 1\\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}\).

Step 3: the same from the formula. \(\mathbf{a}_n\mathbf{b}_n^\top = \begin{pmatrix}1\cdot1 & 1\cdot1\\ 0\cdot1 & 0\cdot1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 1\\ 0 & 0\end{pmatrix}\), and \(\mathbf{b}_n\mathbf{a}_n^\top\) is its transpose, \(\begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}\). The sum is \(\begin{pmatrix}2 & 1\\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}\). Same.

Step 4: trace and determinant. Trace \(2 + 0 = 2\). Determinant \(2\times0 - 1\times1 = -1\).

Step 5: eigenvalues. Two numbers that add to the trace and multiply to the determinant solve \(\omega^2 - 2\omega - 1 = 0\), where \(\omega\) stands for the unknown eigenvalue. So \(\omega = \big(2 \pm \sqrt{4 + 4}\big)/2 = 1 \pm \sqrt2\): 2.414 and −0.414. One up, one down: a saddle.

Step 6: the general pattern. For any two reads in a plane, the trace of \(\mathbf{a}_n\mathbf{b}_n^\top + \mathbf{b}_n\mathbf{a}_n^\top\) is \(2\,\mathbf{a}_n\cdot\mathbf{b}_n\), and its determinant is \((\mathbf{a}_n\cdot\mathbf{b}_n)^2 - \lVert\mathbf{a}_n\rVert^2\lVert\mathbf{b}_n\rVert^2\). The same quadratic then gives eigenvalues \(\mathbf{a}_n\cdot\mathbf{b}_n \pm \lVert\mathbf{a}_n\rVert\lVert\mathbf{b}_n\rVert\). Here \(\mathbf{a}_n\cdot\mathbf{b}_n = 1\), \(\lVert\mathbf{a}_n\rVert = 1\), \(\lVert\mathbf{b}_n\rVert = \sqrt2\): \(1 \pm \sqrt2\) again. The determinant is never positive (a dot product is at most the product of the lengths), so one eigenvalue is \(\ge 0\) and the other \(\le 0\).

What is fixed and what is a convention (theory-v2 Remark 2.1)

  • Fixed under any change of basis (Sylvester's law of inertia, Level 9): a threshold site is rank 1 and one-signed; a product site has eigenvalues \(\bar{g}_n(\mathbf{a}_n\cdot\mathbf{b}_n \pm \lVert\mathbf{a}_n\rVert\lVert\mathbf{b}_n\rVert)\), one positive and one negative (inertia (1,1)); it is rank 1 if \(\mathbf{a}_n\) and \(\mathbf{b}_n\) are parallel.
  • A convention: which curvature you call “product” and which “threshold”. Write SiLU as the product \(z\cdot\sigma(z)\) and the same curvature splits differently: \(\operatorname{silu}'' = 2\sigma' + z\sigma''\), a product part \(2\sigma'\) plus a threshold part \(z\sigma''\). At \(z_1 = -0.5\): \(2\times0.2350 = 0.4700\) plus \(-0.5\times0.0576 = -0.0288\), total 0.4412. Same total, different labels. theory-v2 uses the native graph of the weights for SwiGLU (next point) and says so.
  • SwiGLU (theory-v2 §2.1; Level 14): each MLP neuron computes \(\operatorname{silu}(\text{gate read})\times(\text{up read})\), a single product site. Its curvature has a gate×up block with coefficient involving \(\operatorname{silu}'\), and a rank-1 gate×gate block whose coefficient involves \(\operatorname{silu}''\) times the up read. The directions are fixed by the weights; the coefficients depend on the input.

12.6 Interactions at finite size: the four corners

Level 6 measured an interaction with four corners. theory-v2 §3 writes it with the “both moves applied” point as the corner \(\mathbf{h}\), and the moves \(\mathbf{a}\), \(\mathbf{b}\) taken away from it:

$$ I(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}) = f(\mathbf{h}) - f(\mathbf{h}-\mathbf{a}) - f(\mathbf{h}-\mathbf{b}) + f(\mathbf{h}-\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}). $$

Read it as: (effect of \(\mathbf{a}\) when \(\mathbf{b}\) is present) minus (effect of \(\mathbf{a}\) when \(\mathbf{b}\) is absent). Farm check (Level 6): \(\mathbf{h} = (5\ \text{buckets}, 3\ \text{bags})\), \(\mathbf{a} = (1\ \text{bucket}, 0)\), \(\mathbf{b} = (0, 1\ \text{bag})\).

Step 1: the corners. \(\mathbf{h} - \mathbf{a} = (4, 3)\), \(\mathbf{h} - \mathbf{b} = (5, 2)\), \(\mathbf{h} - \mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b} = (4, 2)\).

Step 2: the yields, from \(Y = 2W + 3F + 0.5WF - 0.25W^2 - 0.5F^2\). \(Y(5,3) = 10 + 9 + 7.5 - 6.25 - 4.5 = 15.75\). \(Y(4,3) = 8 + 9 + 6 - 4 - 4.5 = 14.5\). \(Y(5,2) = 10 + 6 + 5 - 6.25 - 2 = 12.75\). \(Y(4,2) = 8 + 6 + 4 - 4 - 2 = 12\).

Step 3: combine with signs \(+, -, -, +\). \(15.75 - 14.5 - 12.75 + 12 = 0.5\) bushels. The same as Level 6, because the four corners are the same.

For a quadratic function, \(I = \mathbf{a}^\top H\mathbf{b}\) exactly. For anything else, which Hessian? At \(\mathbf{h}\)? At the far corner? In the middle? Lemma 3 answers: none of them; the average over the whole parallelogram.

12.7 The rectangle identity (theory-v2 Lemma 3)

Two tools: integrals as averages, and the fundamental theorem

An integral \(\int_0^1 q(s)\,ds\) is the continuous version of a sum: chop \([0,1]\) into many thin pieces of width \(\Delta s\), add up \(q(s)\,\Delta s\) over the pieces, and shrink \(\Delta s\). Over an interval of length 1, that is exactly the average value of \(q\). (Here \(q\) is any smooth function of one variable.)

The fundamental theorem of calculus says: adding up a rate gives the total change. For any smooth function \(q\) of one variable,

$$ \int_0^1 q'(s)\,ds = q(1) - q(0). $$

Real-world example. The car of Level 2 has position \(t^2\) metres and speed \(2t\) m/s. Adding up its speed over 3 seconds, \(\int_0^3 2t\,dt\), gives 9 metres, which is exactly \(3^2 - 0^2\): how far it went. The odometer integrates the speedometer. A double integral over the unit square, \(\int_0^1\!\int_0^1 \ldots\,ds\,dt\), is likewise the average over the square, whose area is 1.

Lemma 3 (the rectangle identity) [standard; proved] Let \(f\) have continuous second derivatives near the parallelogram \(P = \{\mathbf{h} - \mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b} + s\mathbf{a} + t\mathbf{b} : 0\le s,t\le1\}\). Then $$ I(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}) = \int_0^1\!\!\int_0^1 \mathbf{a}^\top\,\nabla^2 f(\mathbf{h} - \mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b} + s\mathbf{a} + t\mathbf{b})\,\mathbf{b}\;ds\,dt \;=\; \mathbf{a}^\top\bar{H}_P\,\mathbf{b}. $$ The four-corner interaction equals the Hessian averaged over the parallelogram, read along \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\). Exactly: no Taylor series, no small-move assumption.

theory-v2 §3 is explicit that this is not new: it is the integral form of the classical mixed-difference mean-value theorem (Rudin, Principles of Mathematical Analysis, Thm 9.40), and the second-order analogue of the completeness property of Integrated Gradients (Sundararajan et al. 2017). “It is a tool here, not a contribution.”

The proof, every step

  1. Walk around the parallelogram with two numbers. Let \(\psi(s,t) = f(\mathbf{h} - \mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b} + s\mathbf{a} + t\mathbf{b})\). The corners are \(\psi(1,1) = f(\mathbf{h})\), \(\psi(0,1) = f(\mathbf{h}-\mathbf{a})\), \(\psi(1,0) = f(\mathbf{h}-\mathbf{b})\), \(\psi(0,0) = f(\mathbf{h}-\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b})\). So $$ I = \big[\psi(1,1) - \psi(0,1)\big] - \big[\psi(1,0) - \psi(0,0)\big]. $$
  2. Name the bracket: \(\operatorname{gap}(t) = \psi(1,t) - \psi(0,t)\), the difference between the right and left edges at height \(t\). Then \(I = \operatorname{gap}(1) - \operatorname{gap}(0)\).
  3. Fundamental theorem in \(t\): \(I = \int_0^1 \operatorname{gap}'(t)\,dt = \int_0^1\big[\partial_t\psi(1,t) - \partial_t\psi(0,t)\big]dt\).
  4. Fundamental theorem again, now in \(s\), on the bracket: \(\partial_t\psi(1,t) - \partial_t\psi(0,t) = \int_0^1 \partial_s\partial_t\psi(s,t)\,ds\). So \(I = \int_0^1\!\int_0^1 \partial_s\partial_t\psi\,ds\,dt\).
  5. Compute \(\partial_t\psi\) by the chain rule. Moving \(t\) moves the point along \(\mathbf{b}\), so \(\partial_t\psi = \nabla f(\text{point})\cdot\mathbf{b} = \sum_l \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_l}\,b_l\).
  6. Compute \(\partial_s\) of that. Moving \(s\) moves the point along \(\mathbf{a}\), so each \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_l}\) changes at rate \(\sum_i \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i\partial x_l}a_i\). Hence \(\partial_s\partial_t\psi = \sum_{i,l}a_i\,\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i\,\partial x_l}\,b_l = \mathbf{a}^\top\nabla^2 f(\text{point})\,\mathbf{b}\).
  7. Substitute into step 4. ∎

Worked example on the tiny network

Let \(f\) be the tiny network's output, with corner \(\mathbf{h} = (1, 2)\), \(\mathbf{a} = (1, -1)\), \(\mathbf{b} = (0, 1)\). These are large moves for this network.

Step 1: the corners. \(\mathbf{h} = (1,2)\). \(\mathbf{h}-\mathbf{a} = (1-1,\ 2+1) = (0,3)\). \(\mathbf{h}-\mathbf{b} = (1,\ 2-1) = (1,1)\). \(\mathbf{h}-\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b} = (0,\ 3-1) = (0,2)\).

Step 2: forward passes (Level 10), with \(z_1 = x_1 - x_2 + 0.5\) and \(z_2 = 0.5x_1 + x_2 - 1\). At \((1,2)\): \(z = (-0.5, 1.5)\), \(f = -1.5095\). At \((0,3)\): \(z = (-2.5, 2)\), \(f = -2.0461\). At \((1,1)\): \(z = (0.5, 0.5)\), \(f = 0.1556\). At \((0,2)\): \(z = (-1.5, 1)\), \(f = -1.1415\).

Step 3: the interaction. Signs \(+, -, -, +\): \(I = -1.5095 - (-2.0461) - 0.1556 + (-1.1415) = -1.5095 + 2.0461 - 0.1556 - 1.1415 = -0.7605\), which is \(-0.7606\) from unrounded values.

Step 4: the reads. \(\mathbf{w}_1\cdot\mathbf{a} = 1\times1 + (-1)(-1) = 2\). \(\mathbf{w}_1\cdot\mathbf{b} = 1\times0 + (-1)\times1 = -1\). \(\mathbf{w}_2\cdot\mathbf{a} = 0.5\times1 + 1\times(-1) = -0.5\). \(\mathbf{w}_2\cdot\mathbf{b} = 0.5\times0 + 1\times1 = 1\).

Step 5: the Hessian read along \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\), at any point \(\mathbf{x}\). From 12.2, \(H = \sum_i c_i\operatorname{silu}''(z_i)\,\mathbf{w}_i\mathbf{w}_i^\top\). Sandwiching an outer product gives a product of dot products: \(\mathbf{a}^\top\mathbf{w}_i\mathbf{w}_i^\top\mathbf{b} = (\mathbf{w}_i\cdot\mathbf{a})(\mathbf{w}_i\cdot\mathbf{b})\). Put in Step 4:

$$ \mathbf{a}^\top H(\mathbf{x})\,\mathbf{b} = 1.5\operatorname{silu}''(z_1)(2)(-1) + (-1)\operatorname{silu}''(z_2)(-0.5)(1) = -3\operatorname{silu}''(z_1) + 0.5\operatorname{silu}''(z_2). $$

Step 6: at the corner \(\mathbf{h}\) (\(z = (-0.5, 1.5)\), \(\operatorname{silu}'' = 0.4412\) and \(0.1562\)): \(-3\times0.44123 + 0.5\times0.15620 = -1.3237 + 0.0781 = -1.2456\). It misses \(I\) by \((1.2456 - 0.7606)/0.7606 = 64\%\).

Step 7: at the centre. \(\mathbf{m} = \mathbf{h} - (\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b})/2 = (1,2) - (0.5, 0) = (0.5, 2)\). There \(z_1 = 0.5 - 2 + 0.5 = -1\) and \(z_2 = 0.25 + 2 - 1 = 1.25\), with \(\operatorname{silu}''(-1) = 0.3024\) and \(\operatorname{silu}''(1.25) = 0.2262\). So \(-3\times0.30237 + 0.5\times0.22620 = -0.9071 + 0.1131 = -0.7940\). It misses by \((0.7940 - 0.7606)/0.7606 = 4\%\).

Step 8: the point inside \(P\) at \((s, t)\). \(\mathbf{h} - \mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b} + s\mathbf{a} + t\mathbf{b} = (0, 2) + s(1, -1) + t(0, 1) = (s,\ 2 - s + t)\).

Step 9: the pre-activations there. \(z_1 = s - (2 - s + t) + 0.5 = -1.5 + 2s - t\). \(z_2 = 0.5s + (2 - s + t) - 1 = 1 - 0.5s + t\). Check at the centre \(s = t = 0.5\): \(z = (-1, 1.25)\), as in Step 7.

Step 10: average Step 5 over \(P\). A 5×5 Gauss–Legendre rule (below) gives \(-0.760585\), against \(I = -0.760586\). Equal to the rule's accuracy.

Computing the average: Gauss–Legendre quadrature

The average over \(P\) is an integral, and a computer approximates it with a weighted sum at a few points. Gauss–Legendre places \(n_q\) nodes per side at special positions with special weights, chosen so that the sum is exact whenever the integrand is a polynomial of degree up to \(2n_q - 1\) in each variable. On \([0,1]\): one node sits at 0.5 (weight 1); two nodes sit at 0.2113 and 0.7887 (weights 0.5 each); three at 0.1127, 0.5 and 0.8873 (weights 5/18, 8/18, 5/18). The rectangle rule uses the \(n_q\times n_q\) grid of node pairs and multiplies the weights. Each point costs one Hessian evaluation along \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\), which in a real model means an HVP. Real-world example: estimating a field's average height from a few stakes placed where they tell you the most.

Note the one-node rule: a single point at \(s = t = 0.5\). That is the centre Hessian. For the worked example the rules give \(-0.7940\) (1×1), \(-0.7635\) (2×2), \(-0.7603\) (3×3), \(-0.760585\) (5×5).

12.8 Corner against centre: what the gap is made of

theory-v2 §3 gives two expansions of \(I\) for moves that are small but not tiny.

  • Corner expansion: $$ I = \mathbf{a}^\top\nabla^2f(\mathbf{h})\,\mathbf{b} - \tfrac12\Big(D^3f(\mathbf{h})[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}] + D^3f(\mathbf{h})[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{b}]\Big) + O\big(\lVert(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})\rVert^4\big). $$ Where it comes from, step by step.

    Step 1: name the function on the parallelogram. Let \(\chi(\mu,\nu) = f(\mathbf{h} - \mu\mathbf{a} - \nu\mathbf{b})\). The corners are \(\chi(0,0) = f(\mathbf{h})\), \(\chi(1,0) = f(\mathbf{h}-\mathbf{a})\), \(\chi(0,1) = f(\mathbf{h}-\mathbf{b})\), \(\chi(1,1) = f(\mathbf{h}-\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b})\). So \(I = \chi(0,0) - \chi(1,0) - \chi(0,1) + \chi(1,1)\).

    Step 2: Taylor-expand around \(\mathbf{h}\) (Level 4) with the move \(\boldsymbol{\delta} = -\mu\mathbf{a} - \nu\mathbf{b}\):

    $$ \chi(\mu,\nu) = f(\mathbf{h}) + \nabla f\cdot\boldsymbol{\delta} + \tfrac12\,\boldsymbol{\delta}^\top\nabla^2f\,\boldsymbol{\delta} + \tfrac16 D^3f[\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\delta}] + O(\lVert\boldsymbol{\delta}\rVert^4), $$

    with every derivative taken at \(\mathbf{h}\). Each term is a sum of pieces of the form (number) × \(\mu^i\nu^j\).

    Step 3: which pieces survive the four corners. Put a piece \(\mu^i\nu^j\) through \(+\chi(0,0) - \chi(1,0) - \chi(0,1) + \chi(1,1)\). If \(j = 0\) (no \(\nu\)), the piece has the same value at \((0,0)\) and \((0,1)\), and at \((1,0)\) and \((1,1)\); those pairs carry opposite signs, so it cancels. Likewise if \(i = 0\). If \(i, j \ge 1\), the piece is 0 at every corner except \((1,1)\), where it is 1. So \(I\) is the sum of the numbers in front of all pieces that contain both \(\mu\) and \(\nu\). The constant and the first-order term \(\nabla f\cdot\boldsymbol{\delta} = -\mu\,\nabla f\cdot\mathbf{a} - \nu\,\nabla f\cdot\mathbf{b}\) have no such piece.

    Step 4: the second-order term. Multiply out, using \(\mathbf{a}^\top\nabla^2f\,\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{b}^\top\nabla^2f\,\mathbf{a}\) (symmetry): \(\boldsymbol{\delta}^\top\nabla^2f\,\boldsymbol{\delta} = \mu^2\,\mathbf{a}^\top\nabla^2f\,\mathbf{a} + 2\mu\nu\,\mathbf{a}^\top\nabla^2f\,\mathbf{b} + \nu^2\,\mathbf{b}^\top\nabla^2f\,\mathbf{b}\). The two minus signs in \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\) multiply to a plus. Times \(\tfrac12\), the \(\mu\nu\) piece has number \(\mathbf{a}^\top\nabla^2f\,\mathbf{b}\).

    Step 5: the third-order term. \(D^3f\) is linear in each slot and does not care about slot order. Pull out \((-1)^3 = -1\) and multiply out like \((\mu + \nu)^3\): \(D^3f[\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\delta}] = -\big(\mu^3D^3f[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a}] + 3\mu^2\nu\,D^3f[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}] + 3\mu\nu^2\,D^3f[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{b}] + \nu^3D^3f[\mathbf{b},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{b}]\big)\). Times \(\tfrac16\), the \(\mu^2\nu\) and \(\mu\nu^2\) pieces have numbers \(-\tfrac12D^3f[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}]\) and \(-\tfrac12D^3f[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{b}]\).

    Step 6: add the survivors. That gives the corner expansion, with everything from fourth order on in the \(O(\cdot)\). Toy check (Exercise 12.8): \(f = x_1^2x_2\), \(\mathbf{h} = (0,0)\), \(\mathbf{a} = (1,0)\), \(\mathbf{b} = (0,1)\). The Hessian at \(\mathbf{h}\) is 0, \(D^3f[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}] = \partial^3f/\partial x_1^2\partial x_2 = 2\), \(D^3f[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{b}] = 0\). So \(I = 0 - \tfrac12(2 + 0) = -1\), which is the true value.

  • Centre expansion: $$ I = \mathbf{a}^\top\nabla^2 f(\mathbf{m})\,\mathbf{b} + O\big(\lVert(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})\rVert^4\big). $$ Why the cubic terms vanish, step by step.

    Step 1: write the corners around \(\mathbf{m}\). Since \(\mathbf{m} = \mathbf{h} - \tfrac12\mathbf{a} - \tfrac12\mathbf{b}\), we have \(\mathbf{h} = \mathbf{m} + \tfrac12\mathbf{a} + \tfrac12\mathbf{b}\), \(\mathbf{h} - \mathbf{a} = \mathbf{m} - \tfrac12\mathbf{a} + \tfrac12\mathbf{b}\), \(\mathbf{h} - \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{m} + \tfrac12\mathbf{a} - \tfrac12\mathbf{b}\), \(\mathbf{h} - \mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{m} - \tfrac12\mathbf{a} - \tfrac12\mathbf{b}\). Label each corner by two signs: it is \(\mathbf{m} + \tfrac12\epsilon_a\mathbf{a} + \tfrac12\epsilon_b\mathbf{b}\) with \(\epsilon_a, \epsilon_b = \pm1\). Its sign in \(I\) is then \(\epsilon_a\epsilon_b\): \((+)(+) = +\) for \(\mathbf{h}\), \((-)(+) = -\) for \(\mathbf{h}-\mathbf{a}\), and so on.

    Step 2: flip both moves. Replace \((\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})\) by \((-\mathbf{a},-\mathbf{b})\), keeping \(\mathbf{m}\). Corner \((\epsilon_a, \epsilon_b)\) becomes corner \((-\epsilon_a, -\epsilon_b)\), and \((-\epsilon_a)(-\epsilon_b) = \epsilon_a\epsilon_b\): its sign is unchanged. The same four values with the same signs: \(I\) is unchanged.

    Step 3: Taylor-expand every corner around \(\mathbf{m}\). The order-\(n\) part is built from \(n\) copies of the move \(\tfrac12\epsilon_a\mathbf{a} + \tfrac12\epsilon_b\mathbf{b}\), so flipping \((\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})\) multiplies it by \((-1)^n\). By Step 2 the total cannot change. For odd \(n\) the order-\(n\) total must equal its own negative, so it is 0. That removes the first-order and third-order (cubic) terms.

    Step 4: order 0. \(f(\mathbf{m})\times(1 - 1 - 1 + 1) = 0\).

    Step 5: order 2. With the move \(\boldsymbol{\delta} = \tfrac12\epsilon_a\mathbf{a} + \tfrac12\epsilon_b\mathbf{b}\) and \(\epsilon^2 = 1\): \(\tfrac12\boldsymbol{\delta}^\top\nabla^2f(\mathbf{m})\,\boldsymbol{\delta} = \tfrac18\mathbf{a}^\top\nabla^2f\,\mathbf{a} + \tfrac18\mathbf{b}^\top\nabla^2f\,\mathbf{b} + \tfrac14\epsilon_a\epsilon_b\,\mathbf{a}^\top\nabla^2f\,\mathbf{b}\). Multiply by the corner's sign \(\epsilon_a\epsilon_b\) and add the four corners. The first two pieces get \(1 - 1 - 1 + 1 = 0\). The last gets \((\epsilon_a\epsilon_b)^2 = 1\) at every corner, so \(4\times\tfrac14 = 1\) copy of \(\mathbf{a}^\top\nabla^2f(\mathbf{m})\,\mathbf{b}\).

    Step 6: collect. \(I = \mathbf{a}^\top\nabla^2f(\mathbf{m})\,\mathbf{b}\) plus terms of order 4 and higher. Toy check: for \(f = x_1^2x_2\) with the corner and moves of the toy check above, \(\mathbf{m} = (-0.5, -0.5)\) and the mixed partial there is \(2x_1 = -1\): exactly \(I\), because \(f\) is cubic and has no fourth-order part.

So the gap between the corner Hessian and the true interaction is exactly the third- and higher-order content of \(f\) over \(P\) (theory-v2: “the gap between \(\nabla^2f(h)\) and \(\bar{H}_P\) is exactly the third- and higher-order content”). Smallest example (Exercise 12.8): for \(f = x_1^2x_2\), corner \(\mathbf{h} = (0,0)\), \(\mathbf{a} = (1,0)\), \(\mathbf{b} = (0,1)\), the true \(I\) is −1, the corner Hessian says 0, and the centre Hessian says −1 exactly, because \(f\) is cubic.

Intuition pump: judging a hill from one stake You want the average tilt-change over a field. Standing in one corner and measuring there is fine if the field is a perfect bowl. If the field's shape changes across it, the corner is the worst place to stand: you are as far as possible from most of the field. The middle is better. Walking the whole field is exact.

12.9 The toy pattern, and the 0.8B check

The real check (theory-v2 §3, hs_rectangle.py, results/rectangle_08b_L12.json)

Setup, as theory-v2 §3 reports it: Qwen3.5-0.8B with fp32 blocks, layer \(\ell = 12\), objective \(F_c\). The moves are single-position: two complementary random halves (in whitened coordinates, Level 13) of one token's deviation from the mean, so \(\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b}\) is that whole deviation (\(\lambda = 1\): natural move size). 8 draws × 4 covectors; the covectors share each draw, so the effective sample size is about 8. Confidence intervals are a bootstrap over draws. Relative L1 error is the sum of absolute misses divided by the sum of absolute interactions (as computed in hs_rectangle.py).   • The rectangle row tests the code, not the model. Lemma 3 is exact, so 0.98 checks that the HVP code and the quadrature are right.
  • The 5×5 rule looks under-resolved. The two worst draws carry 74% of the absolute error.   • A larger run agrees on sign. With 32 draws at the same layer and move size (hs_integrated.py), the corner Hessian gave \(r = 0.29\) and a uniform 4-node diagonal \(r = 0.57\), both with negative explained variance.

In plain words: the Hessian at one point is a bad predictor of interactions at natural move sizes. This is the warning seeded at Level 4. theory-v2 adds (Implication 2) that the earlier v1 failure of the quadratic model at natural move sizes is the same statement as “third- and higher-order terms are large over the rectangle”; the lemma restates the failure, it does not explain it, and v1's own setting was not re-tested.

12.10 Why this matters for H-space

  1. Lemma 1 defines what is being measured. The per-position H-lens (Level 15) is built from the diagonal block, for one position, of \(\nabla^2_{\mathbf{h}}F_c = \sum_k J_k^\top C_kJ_k\), exact for each context. Its averages over contexts are sums of averages, not products of averaged pieces. Splitting it by sublayer is how theory-v2 §2.4 measures which parts of the model create the curvature.
  2. Lemma 2 says where to look. Each SwiGLU neuron is a product site, silu(gate read) × (up read), whose directions are fixed by the weights. That is why the MLP is a natural place to look for interpretable curvature (Level 14).
  3. Lemma 3 says what an interaction is. A finite 2×2 interaction of moves that add at layer \(\ell\) equals a pair-specific, rectangle-averaged Hessian (theory-v2 §3, Implication 1; it needs the joint move to equal \(\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b}\) exactly). The v9 test (Level 15) measures four-corner interactions of one-standard-deviation moves: numbers of this kind, not Hessians at a point.
  4. And what it does not license. theory-v2 §3 Implication 4: the lemma is exact only for the pair-specific average; it does not imply that removing H-space removes interaction energy. That is an empirical question, settled (or not) by the runs on Level 15.
