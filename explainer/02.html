Level 2: Slope and the derivative

An average rate over a shorter and shorter step settles on one number: the rate at an instant. That number is the derivative.

By the end of this level you can
  • compute an average rate of change between two points, and draw it as the slope of a secant line;
  • shrink the step \(\Delta t\) by hand, see the average rate settle, and say what a limit is;
  • define the derivative, read \(f'(x)\) and \(df/dx\) aloud, and state its unit without guessing;
  • derive the derivatives of \(t^2\) and \(t^3\) by expanding, and use the rules for \(t^n\), \(e^x\), sums and constant multiples;
  • compute a derivative numerically, and explain why a very small step can make the answer worse;
  • spot where a derivative does not exist (corners) and why zooming in reveals it.

Prerequisites: Level 0 (rates, \(\Delta\)) and Level 1 (functions, \(f(x+1)\), straight lines, powers, \(e^x\), the car).

2.1 The problem: a rate at one instant

At Level 1 a straight line \(f(x) = m x + b\) had one rate, \(m\), everywhere: every one-unit step added \(m\). The car is not like that. Its position \(s(t) = t^2\) metres gained 1 m in the first second, 3 m in the second, 5 m in the third. There is no single “metres per second” for the whole trip.

Yet a speedometer shows one number at each instant. At exactly \(t = 3\) s it shows some speed. What is it, and how could we compute it? A speed is distance divided by time. In one instant the car covers no distance in no time, and \(0 \div 0\) is not a number. This level resolves that puzzle. The answer is the derivative, and every later level is built on it.

2.2 Average rate over an interval

Start with what we can compute: the average rate over a stretch of time. Let \(t_1\) and \(t_2\) be two times. The car moves \(\Delta s = s(t_2) - s(t_1)\) metres in \(\Delta t = t_2 - t_1\) seconds. Its average speed is

$$ \frac{\Delta s}{\Delta t} = \frac{s(t_2) - s(t_1)}{t_2 - t_1} . $$

This fraction, change in output divided by change in input, is called a difference quotient. It is exactly the “change ÷ change” of Level 0.

Worked example: from 1 s to 3 s

$$ \Delta s = s(3) - s(1) = 9\ \text{m} - 1\ \text{m} = 8\ \text{m}, \qquad \Delta t = 3\ \text{s} - 1\ \text{s} = 2\ \text{s}, \qquad \frac{\Delta s}{\Delta t} = \frac{8\ \text{m}}{2\ \text{s}} = 4\ \text{m/s}. $$

Meaning: a car moving steadily at 4 m/s would cover the same 8 m in the same 2 s. The real car went slower than 4 m/s at the start of the stretch and faster at the end; 4 m/s is the average speed.

Real-world example. Average-speed cameras on motorways photograph a car at two points a known distance apart and divide that distance by the time between photos. They measure exactly this difference quotient, over a long stretch.

Mechanics: a formula for every interval of the car

For the car we can do the division once, for all \(t_1\) and \(t_2\). Use the fact \(t_2^2 - t_1^2 = (t_2 - t_1)(t_2 + t_1)\). (Check by multiplying out: \((t_2 - t_1)(t_2 + t_1) = t_2^2 + t_2 t_1 - t_1 t_2 - t_1^2 = t_2^2 - t_1^2\).) Then

$$ \frac{s(t_2) - s(t_1)}{t_2 - t_1} = \frac{t_2^2 - t_1^2}{t_2 - t_1} = \frac{(t_2 - t_1)(t_2 + t_1)}{t_2 - t_1} = t_1 + t_2 . $$

With the hidden unit of Level 1, \(1\ \text{m/s}^2 \times (t_1 + t_2)\) is in m/s. So the average speed of the car between any two times is just their sum, read in metres per second. Check: \(1 + 3 = 4\) m/s. Mechanics: move the later end one second later and the average speed goes up by exactly 1 m/s.

2.3 The secant line and the word “slope”

Draw the two moments as points on the car's graph: \((t_1, s(t_1))\) and \((t_2, s(t_2))\). The straight line through them is called a secant line. Going from the first point to the second, the line runs \(\Delta t\) across and rises \(\Delta s\) up. Its steepness, rise divided by run, is

$$ m = \frac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}} = \frac{\Delta s}{\Delta t}. $$

That is the \(m\) of a straight line from Level 1, and it is the average speed. From now on we call this steepness the slope. A slope always has a unit: output unit per input unit. Here it is metres per second.

2.4 Shrinking the step: the limit

An average speed over two seconds is a blurry answer to “how fast at 3 s?”. So shrink the stretch. Keep the first end at \(t = 3\) s and let the second end be \(3 + \Delta t\). Compute the average speed for smaller and smaller \(\Delta t\).

By hand, every step

\(\Delta t = 1\) s: \(s(4) = 16\), so \(\dfrac{16 - 9}{1} = 7\) m/s.

\(\Delta t = 0.5\) s: \(s(3.5) = 3.5 \times 3.5 = 12.25\), so \(\dfrac{12.25 - 9}{0.5} = \dfrac{3.25}{0.5} = 6.5\) m/s.

\(\Delta t = 0.1\) s: \(s(3.1) = 9.61\), so \(\dfrac{9.61 - 9}{0.1} = \dfrac{0.61}{0.1} = 6.1\) m/s.

\(\Delta t = 0.01\) s: \(s(3.01) = 9.0601\), so \(\dfrac{0.0601}{0.01} = 6.01\) m/s.

\(\Delta t = -0.1\) s (look backwards): \(s(2.9) = 8.41\), so \(\dfrac{8.41 - 9}{-0.1} = \dfrac{-0.59}{-0.1} = 5.9\) m/s.

From both sides the numbers close in on 6 m/s. The pattern is plain: the average speed is \(6 + \Delta t\).

The algebra behind the table

Feed \(3 + \Delta t\) into \(s\), as at Level 1. First expand the square:

$$ (3 + \Delta t)^2 = (3 + \Delta t)(3 + \Delta t) = 9 + 3\Delta t + 3\Delta t + \Delta t^2 = 9 + 6\,\Delta t + \Delta t^2 . $$

Here \(\Delta t^2\) means \((\Delta t)^2\). Subtract \(s(3) = 9\) and divide by \(\Delta t\):

$$ \frac{s(3 + \Delta t) - s(3)}{\Delta t} = \frac{9 + 6\,\Delta t + \Delta t^2 - 9}{\Delta t} = \frac{6\,\Delta t + \Delta t^2}{\Delta t} = 6 + \Delta t \qquad (\Delta t \ne 0). $$

The last step divides top and bottom by \(\Delta t\). That is only allowed because \(\Delta t\) is not zero. At \(\Delta t = 0\) the original fraction is \(0/0\) and has no value. But for every \(\Delta t\) that is not zero, the average speed is exactly \(6 + \Delta t\), and as \(\Delta t\) shrinks that gets as close to 6 as we like.

The limit The limit of an expression as \(\Delta t \to 0\) is the value it settles towards as \(\Delta t\) gets closer and closer to 0, from both sides, while never being 0. We write $$ \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} (6 + \Delta t) = 6 . $$ Precisely: for any tolerance you name, say 0.001 m/s, there is a step size below which the average speed stays within that tolerance of 6. Here any \(\lvert\Delta t\rvert\) below 0.001 s works.
Intuition pump: shorter and shorter camera exposures Photograph a moving car with a one-second exposure and you get a long smear: the motion of the whole second, blurred together. Use 1/100 s and the smear is short. Use 1/10000 s and the car is frozen, yet a tiny smear remains, pointing the way it moves. The derivative is what that smear's direction settles on as the exposure shrinks. You never need an exposure of exactly zero.

2.5 The derivative

Do the same for any function \(f\) at any input \(x\). The derivative of \(f\) at \(x\) is

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)}{\Delta x} . $$

In words: the average rate of change over a step \(\Delta x\), in the limit of a tiny step. It is the instantaneous rate of change: the rate at one input, not over a stretch. If this limit exists, \(f\) is called differentiable at \(x\).

Notation and units

  • \(f'(x)\) is read “f prime of x”. The prime mark says “derivative of”.
  • \(\dfrac{df}{dx}\) is read “d f d x”. It is a reminder of where the derivative came from: \(\Delta f / \Delta x\) with the steps made tiny. It is not a fraction you can compute directly, but its units behave like one.
  • Unit of a derivative: output unit per input unit, always. The derivative of position in metres with respect to time in seconds is in metres per second. The derivative of a cost in dollars with respect to a mass in kilograms is in dollars per kilogram.

Worked example: the car's speed at every instant

Repeat Section 2.4 with a general time \(t\) instead of 3. Expand \((t + \Delta t)^2 = t^2 + 2t\,\Delta t + \Delta t^2\). Then

$$ \frac{s(t + \Delta t) - s(t)}{\Delta t} = \frac{t^2 + 2t\,\Delta t + \Delta t^2 - t^2}{\Delta t} = \frac{2t\,\Delta t + \Delta t^2}{\Delta t} = 2t + \Delta t . $$

Let \(\Delta t \to 0\). The \(\Delta t\) term vanishes, so

$$ s'(t) = \frac{ds}{dt} = 2t \quad \text{metres per second.} $$

Units. Restore the hidden 1 m/s² from Level 1: \(s(t) = (1\ \text{m/s}^2)\,t^2\), so \(s'(t) = (2\ \text{m/s}^2)\cdot t\). At \(t = 3\) s this is \(2\ \text{m/s}^2 \cdot 3\ \text{s} = 6\ \text{m/s}\). The seconds cancel once and leave m/s. With the physicists' form \(s(t) = \tfrac12 a t^2\) the same steps give \(s'(t) = \tfrac12 a \cdot 2t = a t\), and \(a = 2\) m/s².

At 10 s the car does 20 m/s, which is \(20 \times 3.6 = 72\) km/h. The derivative is itself a function: feed in a time, get out a speed. Its graph is a straight line through the origin, rising 2 m/s for every second. Level 3 takes the derivative of this new function.

Mechanics. Change only \(t\), from 3 s to 3.5 s. The speed changes from 6 m/s to 7 m/s. The position changes from 9 m to 12.25 m. Compare with Level 1: there the steps 1 m, 3 m, 5 m, … were the distances covered in whole seconds. By Section 2.2 each one is \(t_1 + t_2\) m/s times 1 s: \(0 + 1 = 1\), \(1 + 2 = 3\), \(2 + 3 = 5\). And \(t_1 + t_2\) is exactly halfway between the speeds \(2t_1\) and \(2t_2\) at the two ends of that second.

2.6 The tangent line, and zooming in

The tangent line at a point is the straight line through that point with slope equal to the derivative there. At \(t = 3\) s the car is at 9 m with speed 6 m/s, so

$$ T(t) = s(3) + s'(3)\cdot(t - 3) = 9 + 6\,(t - 3) \quad \text{metres}. $$

Units: \(9\ \text{m} + 6\ \tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}\cdot(t - 3\ \text{s})\); the seconds cancel and every term is in metres.

Mechanics: how far the curve is from the tangent line

Step a distance \(\Delta t\) away from 3 s. The curve is at \(s(3 + \Delta t) = 9 + 6\Delta t + \Delta t^2\). The tangent line is at \(T(3 + \Delta t) = 9 + 6\Delta t\). The gap is

$$ s(3 + \Delta t) - T(3 + \Delta t) = (9 + 6\Delta t + \Delta t^2) - (9 + 6\Delta t) = \Delta t^2 . $$

Differentiable means “straight when you zoom in” A function is differentiable at a point if zooming in there makes its graph look like a straight line. The slope of that line is the derivative. The leftover gap, \(\Delta t^2\) here, is where the bending lives: Level 3 measures it, and Level 4 uses the tangent line as the first approximation of a function.

2.7 The power rule, derived for \(t^2\) and \(t^3\)

We found that the derivative of \(t^2\) is \(2t\). Now do \(t^3\) the same way, every step shown.

First expand \((t + \Delta t)^3\). Start from the square, \((t + \Delta t)^2 = t^2 + 2t\,\Delta t + \Delta t^2\), and multiply by \((t + \Delta t)\) once more. Multiply each term by \(t\), then each term by \(\Delta t\):

$$ (t^2 + 2t\,\Delta t + \Delta t^2)\cdot t = t^3 + 2t^2\,\Delta t + t\,\Delta t^2 , $$ $$ (t^2 + 2t\,\Delta t + \Delta t^2)\cdot \Delta t = t^2\,\Delta t + 2t\,\Delta t^2 + \Delta t^3 . $$

Add the two lines and collect matching terms:

$$ (t + \Delta t)^3 = t^3 + 3t^2\,\Delta t + 3t\,\Delta t^2 + \Delta t^3 . $$

Now subtract \(t^3\) and divide by \(\Delta t\):

$$ \frac{(t+\Delta t)^3 - t^3}{\Delta t} = \frac{3t^2\,\Delta t + 3t\,\Delta t^2 + \Delta t^3}{\Delta t} = 3t^2 + 3t\,\Delta t + \Delta t^2 . $$

As \(\Delta t \to 0\), the last two terms vanish. So the derivative of \(t^3\) is \(3t^2\).

Numerical check at \(t = 2\) with \(\Delta t = 0.01\): \(2.01^3 = 8.120601\), so \(\dfrac{8.120601 - 8}{0.01} = 12.0601\). The formula \(3t^2 + 3t\,\Delta t + \Delta t^2\) gives \(12 + 0.06 + 0.0001 = 12.0601\). The limit is \(3 \cdot 2^2 = 12\).

The pattern

function\(1\) (a constant)\(t\)\(t^2\)\(t^3\)\(t^n\)
derivative\(0\)\(1\)\(2t\)\(3t^2\)\(n\,t^{n-1}\)

The constant: \(\dfrac{1 - 1}{\Delta t} = 0\) for every step, so its derivative is 0. A flat graph has slope 0. For \(t\): \(\dfrac{(t + \Delta t) - t}{\Delta t} = 1\).

Why the pattern holds for every whole \(n\): when you expand \((t + \Delta t)^n\), the first two terms are always \(t^n + n\,t^{n-1}\Delta t\) (one way to pick no \(\Delta t\), and \(n\) ways to pick exactly one \(\Delta t\) from the \(n\) brackets). Every other term contains \(\Delta t^2\) or a higher power. After you subtract \(t^n\) and divide by \(\Delta t\), those other terms still carry at least one \(\Delta t\), so they vanish in the limit. The rule also holds for negative and fractional exponents, for example \(t^{1/2} = \sqrt{t}\) has derivative \(\tfrac12 t^{-1/2}\); that needs a different proof, which we do not give here.

Power rule $$ \frac{d}{dt}\,t^n = n\,t^{n-1} . $$ Unit check: if \(t\) is in seconds, \(t^n\) is in sⁿ and \(n\,t^{n-1}\) is in sⁿ⁻¹, which is sⁿ per s. Output unit per input unit, as it must be.

2.8 Sums and constant multiples

Two rules let us differentiate any polynomial. Let \(f\) and \(g\) be functions and \(c\) a constant.

Constant multiple. Scaling a function scales its rate of change by the same factor:

$$ \frac{c\,f(x+\Delta x) - c\,f(x)}{\Delta x} = c\cdot\frac{f(x+\Delta x) - f(x)}{\Delta x} \;\to\; c\,f'(x). $$

Sum. The rate of change of a sum is the sum of the rates. Split the fraction into two:

$$ \frac{\big(f(x+\Delta x) + g(x+\Delta x)\big) - \big(f(x) + g(x)\big)}{\Delta x} = \frac{f(x+\Delta x) - f(x)}{\Delta x} + \frac{g(x+\Delta x) - g(x)}{\Delta x} \;\to\; f'(x) + g'(x). $$

Worked example

Let \(f(x) = 3x^2 + 5x + 7\) (toy, pure numbers). Take each term separately, using the power rule and the constant-multiple rule:

$$ f'(x) = 3\cdot 2x + 5\cdot 1 + 0 = 6x + 5 . $$

At \(x = 2\): \(f'(2) = 12 + 5 = 17\). Numerical check with \(\Delta x = 0.001\): \(f(2.001) = 3 \cdot 4.004001 + 10.005 + 7 = 29.017003\) and \(f(2) = 29\), so the quotient is \(0.017003 / 0.001 = 17.003\). Close to 17, and closer for a smaller step.

Mechanics: constants vanish. The 7 contributes nothing to \(f'\). Adding a constant slides the graph up without changing its steepness anywhere, just like the taxi's start fee in Level 0. Changing the 5 to a 6 adds 1 to the slope everywhere. Changing the 3 to a 4 adds \(2x\) to the slope: more at large \(x\), nothing at \(x = 0\).

2.9 The exponential is its own derivative

Level 1 said \(e \approx 2.71828\) is a natural choice. Here is why. Use \(e^{x + \Delta x} = e^x \cdot e^{\Delta x}\):

$$ \frac{e^{x+\Delta x} - e^x}{\Delta x} = \frac{e^x\cdot e^{\Delta x} - e^x}{\Delta x} = e^x \cdot \frac{e^{\Delta x} - 1}{\Delta x} . $$

The factor on the right does not depend on \(x\). Compute it for shrinking steps:

It settles on 1. So

$$ \frac{d}{dx}\,e^x = e^x . $$

The rate of change of \(e^x\) equals its current value. At \(x = 2\), the graph is at height \(e^2 \approx 7.389\) and its slope is also about 7.389. For comparison, the same factor for \(2^x\) settles on \(\log 2 \approx 0.6931\) instead of 1 (with \(\Delta x = 0.001\) it is 0.69339), so \(2^x\) grows at about 0.693 times its own value. \(e\) is the one base where that factor is exactly 1.

Real-world example. A colony of bacteria with unlimited food grows at a rate proportional to how many bacteria there are: twice the bacteria, twice the new bacteria per hour. Quantities like that follow an exponential curve.

2.10 Numerical derivative versus exact

A computer can approximate a derivative without any algebra: pick a small \(\Delta x\) and compute the difference quotient. This is called a forward difference. How good is it?

The error from the step size

For the car, Section 2.5 showed the forward difference is exactly \(2t + \Delta t\). The error is \(\Delta t\): a step of 0.01 s gives an answer 0.01 m/s too high. For \(t^3\), Section 2.7 showed the error is \(3t\,\Delta t + \Delta t^2\). Either way the error shrinks in proportion to the step.

A better recipe looks both ways. The central difference is

$$ \frac{f(x + \Delta x) - f(x - \Delta x)}{2\,\Delta x} . $$

For \(t^3\), expand both cubes (the second is the first with \(-\Delta t\) in place of \(\Delta t\)):

$$ (t+\Delta t)^3 - (t - \Delta t)^3 = \big(t^3 + 3t^2\Delta t + 3t\Delta t^2 + \Delta t^3\big) - \big(t^3 - 3t^2\Delta t + 3t\Delta t^2 - \Delta t^3\big) = 6t^2\,\Delta t + 2\,\Delta t^3 . $$

Divide by \(2\Delta t\): the central difference is \(3t^2 + \Delta t^2\). Its error is \(\Delta t^2\). Shrink the step 10 times and the forward error shrinks 10 times, but the central error shrinks 100 times. For \(t^2\) the central difference is exact: \(\big((t+\Delta t)^2 - (t-\Delta t)^2\big)/(2\Delta t) = 4t\Delta t / (2\Delta t) = 2t\).

The error from rounding

So why not use \(\Delta x = 10^{-15}\)? Because a computer keeps only about 16 significant digits. When \(\Delta x\) is tiny, \(f(x + \Delta x)\) and \(f(x)\) agree in almost all their digits. Subtracting them throws those digits away, and what is left is mostly rounding noise. Dividing by the tiny \(\Delta x\) then blows the noise up. This is a rounding error.

Concretely, for \(e^x\) at \(x = 1\) in ordinary double precision: the forward difference has error about \(1.4\times10^{-2}\) at \(\Delta x = 10^{-2}\), about \(7\times10^{-9}\) at \(\Delta x = 10^{-8}\), and about \(4\times10^{-4}\) at \(\Delta x = 10^{-12}\). Smaller is not always better. Figure 2.5 computes these numbers live in your browser.

2.11 When derivatives fail: corners

Not every function has a derivative everywhere. The simplest failure is the absolute value \(\lvert x \rvert\) at \(x = 0\). Its graph is a V with a sharp corner at the origin.

Compute the difference quotient at 0. For a step \(\Delta x\) to the right (\(\Delta x\) positive), \(\lvert \Delta x \rvert = \Delta x\):

$$ \frac{\lvert 0 + \Delta x\rvert - \lvert 0 \rvert}{\Delta x} = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta x} = 1 . $$

For a step to the left (\(\Delta x\) negative), \(\lvert \Delta x \rvert = -\Delta x\):

$$ \frac{\lvert \Delta x\rvert - 0}{\Delta x} = \frac{-\Delta x}{\Delta x} = -1 . $$
\(\Delta x\)0.10.0010.00001−0.00001−0.001−0.1
quotient111−1−1−1

From the right it is always 1; from the left always −1. However small the step, the two sides disagree. The quotient never settles on one value, so the limit does not exist, and \(\lvert x \rvert\) has no derivative at 0. Away from 0 there is no problem: near \(x = 2\), \(\lvert x \rvert = x\) and the derivative is 1; near \(x = -2\), \(\lvert x\rvert = -x\) and the derivative is \(-1\).

The zoom test of Figure 2.4 shows the same thing: a corner looks identical at every magnification. A function with a jump, such as a price that steps from 1 $ to 2 $ at midnight, also has no derivative at the jump.

Real-world example. Roll a ball at 1 m/s straight at a wall and let it bounce back at 1 m/s. Its distance from the wall, graphed over time, is a V with its corner at the moment of the bounce. Just before, the distance shrinks at 1 m/s; just after, it grows at 1 m/s. At the instant itself there is no single rate. A real ball squashes for a moment, so the real graph is rounded at very small scales; the corner is the idealisation.

Preview. The ReLU used in many neural networks gives 0 for negative inputs and \(x\) itself for positive ones. It has exactly this kind of corner at 0 (Level 10). Smooth alternatives such as SiLU have no corner.

2.12 Why this matters for H-space

  1. Everything at the top is a derivative. A Jacobian (Level 8) is a table of derivatives of several outputs with respect to several inputs. The J-lens is an average Jacobian of a transformer's final residual state with respect to an earlier layer (theory-v2 §0, Level 14). H-space goes one derivative higher (Level 3, Level 9).
  2. Units decide what a number means. A derivative is output unit per input unit. When the inputs are measured in units of their natural spread (whitening, Level 13), the same function has different derivative numbers. Section 2.5's habit of tracking units is what keeps that straight.
  3. Numerical versus exact. Level 11 checks an exact Hessian-vector product against a central finite difference, and faces the same trade-off as Figure 2.5: too large a step gives step error, too small a step gives rounding error, and low-precision formats such as bf16 make the rounding much worse.
  4. Corners matter for second derivatives. The MLPs of the Qwen models use SwiGLU, built on the smooth SiLU, not on ReLU (Level 14). Smoothness is what lets the second derivatives of the next level exist at every point.

Exercises

Exercise 2.1. (The car: its position is \(s(t) = t^2\) metres at time \(t\) seconds.) What is the car's average speed between \(t_1 = 2\) s and \(t_2 = 6\) s? Compute it from the positions, then check with the shortcut \(t_1 + t_2\).

Show answer

\(\Delta s = s(6) - s(2) = 36 - 4 = 32\) m and \(\Delta t = 4\) s, so \(\Delta s / \Delta t = 32\ \text{m} / 4\ \text{s} = 8\) m/s. Shortcut: \(2 + 6 = 8\) m/s.

Exercise 2.2. (The car: its position is \(s(t) = t^2\) metres at time \(t\) seconds.) Compute the car's difference quotient at \(t = 5\) s with \(\Delta t = 0.1\) s. What does it settle on as \(\Delta t \to 0\)?

Show answer

\(s(5.1) = 5.1 \times 5.1 = 26.01\) m and \(s(5) = 25\) m. The quotient is \((26.01 - 25)/0.1 = 1.01/0.1 = 10.1\) m/s. By Section 2.5 it equals \(2t + \Delta t = 10 + 0.1\), which settles on \(s'(5) = 10\) m/s.

Exercise 2.3. Let \(f(x) = 4x^3\) (pure numbers). Find \(f'(x)\) and then \(f'(2)\).

Show answer

Constant multiple and power rule: \(f'(x) = 4 \cdot 3x^2 = 12x^2\). At \(x = 2\): \(12 \cdot 4 = 48\).

Exercise 2.4. Let \(f(x) = 3x^2 - 2x + 5\). Find \(f'(1)\).

Show answer

Term by term: \(f'(x) = 3\cdot 2x - 2\cdot 1 + 0 = 6x - 2\). At \(x = 1\): \(6 - 2 = 4\). The constant 5 does not affect the slope.

Exercise 2.5. Derive the derivative of \(t^4\) by expanding, as in Section 2.7. Use \((t+\Delta t)^4 = t^4 + 4t^3\Delta t + 6t^2\Delta t^2 + 4t\Delta t^3 + \Delta t^4\) (or check it by multiplying \((t+\Delta t)^3\) by \((t + \Delta t)\)). Enter the derivative at \(t = 1\).

Show answer

Subtract \(t^4\) and divide by \(\Delta t\): \(4t^3 + 6t^2\Delta t + 4t\Delta t^2 + \Delta t^3\). As \(\Delta t \to 0\) only \(4t^3\) survives. At \(t = 1\) that is 4. This matches the power rule \(n\,t^{n-1}\) with \(n = 4\).

Exercise 2.6. (The car: its position is \(s(t) = t^2\) metres at time \(t\) seconds.) Write the tangent line to the car's graph at \(t = 4\) s. How far is the curve above the tangent line at \(t = 4.5\) s?

Show answer

\(s(4) = 16\) m and \(s'(4) = 8\) m/s, so \(T(t) = 16 + 8(t - 4)\) metres. At 4.5 s: \(T = 16 + 8 \cdot 0.5 = 20\) m, while \(s(4.5) = 20.25\) m. The gap is \(0.25\) m, which is \(\Delta t^2 = 0.5^2\), as in Section 2.6.

Exercise 2.7. A tank drains so that the water left after \(t\) minutes is \(200 - 3t^2\) litres (a toy). What is the unit of its derivative, and what is the derivative at \(t = 2\) min? What does the sign mean?

Show answer

Output unit per input unit: litres per minute. The derivative is \(0 - 3\cdot 2t = -6t\), so at 2 min it is \(-12\) L/min. Negative: the water level is falling, by 12 litres per minute at that instant.

Exercise 2.8. Let \(f(x) = 2e^x + x^2\). Find \(f'(0)\).

Show answer

Sum and constant multiple: \(f'(x) = 2e^x + 2x\). At \(x = 0\): \(2e^0 + 0 = 2 \cdot 1 = 2\).

Exercise 2.9. Compute the central difference for \(f(t) = t^3\) at \(t = 2\) with \(\Delta t = 0.1\). How far is it from the exact derivative 12, and how does that compare with the forward difference?

Show answer

\(2.1^3 = 9.261\) and \(1.9^3 = 6.859\). Central: \((9.261 - 6.859)/0.2 = 2.402/0.2 = 12.01\). Error 0.01, which is \(\Delta t^2\). Forward: \((9.261 - 8)/0.1 = 12.61\), error 0.61, which is \(3t\,\Delta t + \Delta t^2 = 0.6 + 0.01\).

Exercise 2.10. What is the derivative of \(\lvert x \rvert\) at \(x = -3\)? Why does the same method fail at \(x = 0\)?

Show answer

Near \(-3\), all inputs are negative, so \(\lvert x \rvert = -x\) and the slope is \(-1\). At 0 the difference quotient is \(+1\) for every step to the right and \(-1\) for every step to the left, so it never settles on one value: no limit, no derivative.
