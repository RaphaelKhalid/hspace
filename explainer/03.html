Level 3: The second derivative: curvature

The derivative of the derivative says how fast the slope itself changes. That is how much a graph bends, and how wrong a straight line is.

By the end of this level you can
  • take the derivative of a derivative, read \(f''(x)\) and \(d^2 f/dx^2\) aloud, and state the unit (like m/s²);
  • read position, speed and acceleration off three linked graphs, each the slope of the one above;
  • say whether a graph is concave up or concave down from the sign of \(f''\), and find inflection points;
  • measure curvature as the gap between a curve and its tangent line, about \(\tfrac12 f''(x)\,\Delta x^2\);
  • compute a second difference from three function values, and know when it is exact and when rounding ruins it;
  • find critical points and classify them with the second-derivative test.

Prerequisites: Level 1 (functions, the car, parabolas) and Level 2 (difference quotients, the derivative, the tangent line, the power rule).

3.1 The derivative of the derivative

At Level 2 the derivative of the car's position \(s(t) = t^2\) was its speed, \(s'(t) = 2t\) m/s. That speed is itself a function of time. So we can ask how fast it changes. Take its derivative, using the same difference quotient as before:

$$ \frac{s'(t + \Delta t) - s'(t)}{\Delta t} = \frac{2(t + \Delta t) - 2t}{\Delta t} = \frac{2t + 2\Delta t - 2t}{\Delta t} = \frac{2\,\Delta t}{\Delta t} = 2 . $$

There is not even a leftover \(\Delta t\) to send to zero: the answer is 2 for every step. So

$$ s''(t) = 2\ \text{m/s}^2 \quad \text{at every time } t. $$

This is the second derivative: the derivative of the derivative. For the car it is the acceleration, the rate of change of speed. Check with a table of speeds from Level 2: 0, 2, 4, 6, 8 m/s at \(t = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4\) s. Each second the speed rises by 2 m/s. That is what “2 m/s²” means: 2 metres per second, gained every second.

With the physicists' form \(s(t) = \tfrac12 a t^2\), Level 2 gave \(s'(t) = a t\). One more derivative gives \(s''(t) = a\). So the \(a = 2\) m/s² of Level 1 is exactly the second derivative. That is why it is called the acceleration.

Notation

  • \(f''(x)\), read “f double prime of x”: two prime marks, two derivatives.
  • \(\dfrac{d^2 f}{dx^2}\), read “d two f d x squared”. It is shorthand for \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\Big(\dfrac{df}{dx}\Big)\). The 2 on the top says “differentiate twice”. The \(dx^2\) on the bottom means \((dx)^2\): you divided by an input step twice. That is where the unit comes from.
Intuition pump: what you feel in a car In a car at a steady 100 km/h on a straight motorway you feel nothing; you could pour a cup of tea. You feel pushed into your seat only while the car speeds up, and thrown forward only while it brakes. Your body measures the second derivative of position, not the first. Our car presses you back with the same steady push for all ten seconds, because \(s''\) is the constant 2 m/s².

3.2 The unit of a second derivative

A derivative has unit “output per input”. A second derivative divides by the input once more: “output per input per input”.

$$ s''(t):\quad \frac{\text{m/s}}{\text{s}} = \frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}\cdot\text{s}} = \frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} . $$

Other examples: if a cost in dollars depends on a number of items, its second derivative is in dollars per item per item. If a crop yield in bushels depends on water in buckets, its second derivative is in bushels per bucket² (Level 5 uses exactly this farm).

Mechanics: what the number does

An acceleration of 2 m/s² changes the speed by 2 m/s for each second. Over a step \(\Delta t\) the speed changes by about \(s''\cdot\Delta t\). With \(\Delta t = 0.5\) s: \(2\ \text{m/s}^2 \times 0.5\ \text{s} = 1\ \text{m/s}\). Check: \(s'(3) = 6\) and \(s'(3.5) = 7\) m/s. The seconds cancel once and leave m/s.

Mechanics: change the unit of the input

Measure time in minutes instead of seconds. One second is \(\tfrac{1}{60}\) minute, so “per second” is “60 per minute”, and “per second per second” is “\(60 \times 60 = 3600\) per minute per minute”:

$$ 6\ \frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} = 6 \times 60\ \frac{\text{m}}{\text{min}} = 360\ \frac{\text{m}}{\text{min}}, \qquad 2\ \frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} = 2 \times 60^2\ \frac{\text{m}}{\text{min}^2} = 7200\ \frac{\text{m}}{\text{min}^2} . $$

The motion did not change. The numbers did. Stretch the input unit by a factor of 60 and the first derivative is multiplied by 60, but the second derivative by \(60^2\). This is not a curiosity. At Level 13 and Level 15, H-space measures curvature in units of each direction's natural spread instead of raw units, and this squared rescaling is exactly what changes which directions look most curved.

3.3 Position, speed and acceleration together

For the car, the acceleration never changes, which makes its third graph a flat line. To see a second derivative that changes, here is a second toy trip. A car leaves one stop sign, speeds up, then brakes and stops exactly at the next stop sign, 50 m away, after 10 s. Its position is

$$ q(t) = 1.5\,t^2 - 0.1\,t^3 \quad \text{metres}, \qquad 0 \le t \le 10\ \text{s}. $$

(Hidden units, as with the car: the 1.5 is in m/s² and the 0.1 is in m/s³, so that each term comes out in metres.) Differentiate term by term with the power rule of Level 2:

$$ q'(t) = 1.5\cdot 2t - 0.1\cdot 3t^2 = 3t - 0.3\,t^2 \ \text{m/s}, \qquad q''(t) = 3 - 0.3\cdot 2t = 3 - 0.6\,t \ \text{m/s}^2 . $$

Reading stacked graphs The height of each graph is the slope of the graph above it. Speed is the slope of position. Acceleration is the slope of speed, and so it is the rate at which the slope of position changes.

3.4 Concave up and concave down

The sign of the second derivative says which way a graph bends.

  • If \(f''(x) \gt 0\), the slope is increasing. The graph bends upward like a cup that holds water. This is called concave up.
  • If \(f''(x) \lt 0\), the slope is decreasing. The graph bends downward like a cap. This is called concave down.
  • If \(f''(x) = 0\) on a whole stretch, the slope does not change there. The graph is straight on that stretch.

Mechanics: watch the slope change

For \(f(x) = x^2\), the slope is \(f'(x) = 2x\). At \(x = -2, -1, 0, 1, 2\) the slopes are \(-4, -2, 0, 2, 4\): rising by 2 per unit step, because \(f''(x) = 2\). Downhill, then less downhill, then flat, then uphill: a cup.

For \(f(x) = -x^2\), the slopes at the same points are \(4, 2, 0, -2, -4\): falling by 2 per unit step, \(f''(x) = -2\). A cap.

Worked example: a curve that does both

Let \(f(x) = x^3 - 3x\) (toy, pure numbers). Then

$$ f'(x) = 3x^2 - 3, \qquad f''(x) = 6x . $$

For negative \(x\), \(6x\) is negative: concave down. For positive \(x\), \(6x\) is positive: concave up. At \(x = 1.5\): \(f'(1.5) = 3 \cdot 2.25 - 3 = 3.75\) and \(f''(1.5) = 9\). Step 0.1 to the right: \(f'(1.6) = 3\cdot 2.56 - 3 = 4.68\). The slope rose by \(4.68 - 3.75 = 0.93\), close to \(f''(1.5) \times 0.1 = 0.9\).

Which side of the tangent line? On a concave-up stretch the curve lies above its tangent lines. On a concave-down stretch it lies below them. Section 3.5 turns that gap into a number.

Real-world examples. Revising for an exam: the first hour helps a lot, the tenth hour helps less. The score as a function of hours is concave down, a pattern called diminishing returns. Savings with compound interest: each year adds more than the year before. The balance as a function of time is concave up.

Intuition pump: driving along the graph Imagine driving along the graph from left to right. On a concave-up stretch the road bends to your left. On a concave-down stretch it bends to your right. On a straight stretch you hold the wheel still. The second derivative says which way, and how hard, the road is turning.

3.5 Curvature: how wrong a straight line is

At Level 2 we compared the car's curve with its tangent line at \(t = 3\) s, \(T(t) = 9 + 6(t - 3)\). After a step \(\Delta t\) the gap was

$$ s(3 + \Delta t) - T(3 + \Delta t) = (9 + 6\Delta t + \Delta t^2) - (9 + 6\Delta t) = \Delta t^2 \ \text{m}. $$

Now look at that gap with the second derivative in hand. \(s'' = 2\) m/s², and \(\tfrac12 s''\,\Delta t^2 = \tfrac12 \cdot 2 \cdot \Delta t^2 = \Delta t^2\). The gap is exactly \(\tfrac12 s''\,\Delta t^2\). Units: \(\text{m/s}^2 \times \text{s}^2 = \text{m}\), as a gap in position should be.

Worked example: the cubic at \(x = 2\)

Take \(f(x) = x^3 - 3x\) again. At \(x = 2\): \(f(2) = 8 - 6 = 2\), \(f'(2) = 12 - 3 = 9\), \(f''(2) = 12\). The tangent line is \(2 + 9\,\Delta x\) after a step \(\Delta x\). Feed \(2 + \Delta x\) into \(f\), using \((2 + \Delta x)^3 = 8 + 12\Delta x + 6\Delta x^2 + \Delta x^3\) (the expansion of Level 2 with \(t = 2\)):

$$ f(2 + \Delta x) = 8 + 12\Delta x + 6\Delta x^2 + \Delta x^3 - 6 - 3\Delta x = 2 + 9\,\Delta x + 6\,\Delta x^2 + \Delta x^3 . $$

Subtract the tangent line \(2 + 9\,\Delta x\):

$$ \text{gap} = 6\,\Delta x^2 + \Delta x^3 . $$

The main part is \(6\,\Delta x^2\), and \(6 = \tfrac12 \cdot 12 = \tfrac12 f''(2)\). The extra \(\Delta x^3\) is smaller still for small steps:

The ratio settles on \(6 = \tfrac12 f''(2)\). The same happens for \(e^x\) at 0, where \(f''(0) = e^0 = 1\): with \(\Delta x = 0.1\), the gap is \(e^{0.1} - 1 - 0.1 \approx 0.005171\), and \(0.005171 / 0.01 \approx 0.517\), close to \(\tfrac12\).

Curvature as the error of the tangent line For a small step \(\Delta x\), $$ f(x + \Delta x) - \big(f(x) + f'(x)\,\Delta x\big) \approx \tfrac12\, f''(x)\, \Delta x^2 . $$ The second derivative measures how wrong the best straight line is, per step squared. A straight line has \(f'' = 0\) everywhere, so its tangent line is never wrong. Level 4 proves the \(\tfrac12\) in general and adds the next correction, the \(\Delta x^3\) term.

Mechanics. Halve the step and the gap shrinks to about a quarter. Double the curvature and the gap doubles. The car's gap is exact at any step size, because \(t^2\) has nothing beyond the \(\Delta t^2\) term. The cubic's is only approximate, because of its \(\Delta x^3\) term.

A note on the word “curvature” Geometers use a stricter curvature, \(\kappa = f''(x) / \big(1 + f'(x)^2\big)^{3/2}\), measured in one over the unit of the axes. A circle that hugs the curve at that point has radius \(1/\kappa\). Where the graph is flat (\(f'(x) = 0\)) the two agree: for \(y = x^2\) at 0, \(\kappa = 2\) and the hugging circle has radius 0.5. Where the graph is steep, \(\kappa\) is smaller in size than \(f''\). On this site, as in the study of Hessians, “curvature” means the second derivative itself.

3.6 The second difference: curvature you can measure

Level 2 approximated a derivative with function values only. The same can be done for the second derivative. Use three values, one step either side:

$$ \frac{f(x + \Delta x) - 2f(x) + f(x - \Delta x)}{\Delta x^2} . $$

This is called the second difference. Where does it come from? It is a difference of two difference quotients: the slope just ahead minus the slope just behind, per step.

$$ \frac{1}{\Delta x}\left( \frac{f(x+\Delta x) - f(x)}{\Delta x} - \frac{f(x) - f(x - \Delta x)}{\Delta x} \right) = \frac{\big(f(x+\Delta x) - f(x)\big) - \big(f(x) - f(x - \Delta x)\big)}{\Delta x^2} = \frac{f(x+\Delta x) - 2f(x) + f(x-\Delta x)}{\Delta x^2}. $$

The slope changing per unit of input is exactly what \(f''\) measures, so as \(\Delta x \to 0\) this settles on \(f''(x)\) (for a smooth function).

Worked example: the car, exact at any step

Expand both squares:

$$ (t + \Delta t)^2 - 2t^2 + (t - \Delta t)^2 = (t^2 + 2t\Delta t + \Delta t^2) - 2t^2 + (t^2 - 2t\Delta t + \Delta t^2) = 2\,\Delta t^2 . $$

The \(t^2\) terms cancel (\(1 - 2 + 1 = 0\)), the \(2t\Delta t\) terms cancel (one plus, one minus), and \(2\Delta t^2\) is left. Divide by \(\Delta t^2\): the second difference is 2 m/s², for every step.

You have seen this before. In the Level 1 table with \(\Delta t = 1\) s, the column “growth of \(\Delta s\)” was 2 m every time. That column was \(s(t+1) - 2s(t) + s(t-1)\). Dividing by \((1\ \text{s})^2\) gives 2 m/s². The acceleration was hiding in the table from the start.

Worked example: the cubic at \(x = 2\)

With \(\Delta x = 0.1\): \(f(2.1) = 9.261 - 6.3 = 2.961\), \(f(2) = 2\), \(f(1.9) = 6.859 - 5.7 = 1.159\).

$$ \frac{2.961 - 2\cdot 2 + 1.159}{0.1^2} = \frac{2.961 - 4 + 1.159}{0.01} = \frac{0.12}{0.01} = 12 . $$

Exactly \(f''(2) = 12\). Why exact? In \(f(x+\Delta x) + f(x - \Delta x)\), every odd power of \(\Delta x\) appears once with a plus and once with a minus, and cancels. For \(x^3\): \((x+\Delta x)^3 - 2x^3 + (x - \Delta x)^3 = 6x\,\Delta x^2\), and \(6x\) is the exact second derivative. So for any polynomial up to \(x^3\) the second difference has no step error at all.

Worked example: \(e^x\) at 0, where it is not exact

It settles on \(e^0 = 1\). The error shrinks about 100 times when the step shrinks 10 times: it goes like \(\Delta x^2\), like the central difference of Level 2.

Rounding is worse here

Now the tiny difference of three nearly equal numbers is divided by \(\Delta x^2\), not \(\Delta x\). Rounding noise is blown up much sooner. For \(e^x\) at \(x = 1\) in ordinary double precision, the error is smallest near \(\Delta x = 10^{-4}\), about \(4\times10^{-8}\). At \(\Delta x = 10^{-8}\) the computed value is off by about 2.7: as large as the answer itself. Figure 3.4 shows this live.

Intuition pump: the tight string Pin a string to the curve at \(x - \Delta x\) and \(x + \Delta x\) and pull it straight. Its midpoint is at height \(\tfrac12\big(f(x - \Delta x) + f(x + \Delta x)\big)\). The second difference is \(2/\Delta x^2\) times how far that midpoint sits above the curve at \(x\). If the curve sags below the string, it is concave up and the second difference is positive. If the curve bulges above the string, it is negative.

3.7 Critical points and the second-derivative test

A critical point is an input where \(f'(x) = 0\): the tangent line is flat. The top of a hill and the bottom of a valley are both critical points. The second derivative tells them apart.

The second-derivative test At a critical point \(x\) (where \(f'(x) = 0\)):
  • if \(f''(x) \gt 0\), the graph is a cup there: \(x\) is a local minimum;
  • if \(f''(x) \lt 0\), the graph is a cap there: \(x\) is a local maximum;
  • if \(f''(x) = 0\), the test says nothing. Look closer.

Why it works. Section 3.5 with \(f'(x) = 0\) says \(f(x + \Delta x) \approx f(x) + \tfrac12 f''(x)\,\Delta x^2\). Near the point the graph looks like a small copy of the Level 1 parabola \(y = c\,x^2\), moved to sit at the critical point, with \(c = \tfrac12 f''(x)\). If \(c \gt 0\), every nearby value is higher: a minimum. If \(c \lt 0\), every nearby value is lower: a maximum.

Worked example: a ball thrown straight up

Let \(z(t) = 20t - 5t^2\) metres be the height of a ball thrown up at 20 m/s (a toy: gravity is rounded to 10 m/s², and air is ignored). The hidden units: 20 m/s and 5 m/s². Then

$$ z'(t) = 20 - 10t\ \ \text{m/s}, \qquad z''(t) = -10\ \text{m/s}^2 . $$

Critical point: \(20 - 10t = 0\), so \(t = 2\) s. There \(z''(2) = -10 \lt 0\): a maximum. The highest point is \(z(2) = 40 - 20 = 20\) m. The second derivative is the pull of gravity, the same at every moment, and negative because it points down.

Worked example: the cubic

For \(f(x) = x^3 - 3x\): \(f'(x) = 3x^2 - 3 = 0\) gives \(x^2 = 1\), so \(x = 1\) or \(x = -1\).

  • At \(x = 1\): \(f''(1) = 6 \gt 0\), a local minimum, with \(f(1) = 1 - 3 = -2\).
  • At \(x = -1\): \(f''(-1) = -6 \lt 0\), a local maximum, with \(f(-1) = -1 + 3 = 2\).

“Local” matters: far to the right the cubic climbs above 2, and far to the left it falls below −2. The test only describes the neighbourhood.

When the test is silent

Three functions with \(f'(0) = 0\) and \(f''(0) = 0\): \(x^3\) has neither a minimum nor a maximum at 0 (it keeps climbing). \(x^4\) has a minimum at 0. \(-x^4\) has a maximum at 0. Same first and second derivatives, three different answers. When \(f'' = 0\), the bending is decided by higher derivatives (Level 4).

3.8 Inflection points

An inflection point is where the graph switches from concave up to concave down, or back. There \(f''\) changes sign.

  • The cubic \(x^3 - 3x\) has \(f''(x) = 6x\), negative to the left of 0 and positive to the right. Inflection at \(x = 0\).
  • The stop-to-stop trip has \(q''(t) = 3 - 0.6t\), positive before 5 s and negative after. Inflection at \(t = 5\) s, the moment the driver moves from the accelerator to the brake. There the slope of the position stops rising and starts falling, so the speed peaks there: 7.5 m/s.
A common mistake \(f''(x) = 0\) does not by itself make an inflection point. \(x^4\) has \(f''(x) = 12x^2\), which is 0 at \(x = 0\) but positive on both sides. No sign change, no inflection: \(x = 0\) is a minimum.

The parabola row explains Level 1's mechanics. There, \(y = c\,x^2\) had steps that grew by \(2c\) per unit step. That growth was the second difference, and it equals the second derivative \(2c\) exactly, because a parabola has no \(\Delta x^3\) term. For the car, \(c = 1\) m/s² and \(s'' = 2c = 2\) m/s².

The last row matters later. For \(\lvert x \rvert\) at 0 the second difference is \(\dfrac{\lvert \Delta x\rvert - 0 + \lvert -\Delta x \rvert}{\Delta x^2} = \dfrac{2}{\lvert \Delta x \rvert}\), which grows without limit as the step shrinks. A function with a corner has zero curvature everywhere except at one point, where the curvature is, in effect, infinitely concentrated. Neural networks built from ReLU have such corners (Level 10).

3.10 Why this matters for H-space

  1. The Hessian is a table of second derivatives. With many inputs, each input has its own second derivative along it, and each pair of inputs has a mixed one (Level 6, Level 9). The entries on the Hessian's diagonal are exactly this level's \(f''\), one per input direction.
  2. The second difference grows into the 2×2 test. Section 3.6 measured curvature from three function values. Level 6 measures interaction from four: \(f(\text{both moves}) - f(\text{first only}) - f(\text{second only}) + f(\text{neither})\). That four-point quantity is what the v9 run measures, in nats, with moves of one standard deviation (Level 15).
  3. “About \(\tfrac12 f''\,\Delta x^2\)” is only about. For small steps the second derivative at one point predicts the gap well. For large steps the \(\Delta x^3\) and higher terms take over (Level 4). In a small check on the 0.8B model at natural move sizes, the Hessian at one corner point predicted the measured interactions with a correlation of −0.09 (about 8 independent draws): no better than chance. A larger run of 32 draws gave 0.29, still poor. The Hessian averaged over the whole rectangle of moves gave 0.98, as an exact theorem says it should (theory-v2 §3, Level 12).
  4. Units are squared. Section 3.2 showed that stretching the input unit by a factor multiplies the second derivative by that factor squared. Whitening measures each direction in units of its natural spread, “curvature per unit of natural activation scale” (theory-v2 §0). That choice changes which directions have the largest curvature.

Change in speed \(\approx s'' \cdot \Delta t = 2\ \text{m/s}^2 \times 0.5\ \text{s} = 1\) m/s, so the speed is \(8 + 1 = 9\) m/s. Check: \(s'(4.5) = 9\) m/s. Exact here because \(s''\) is constant.

Exercise 3.2. Let \(f(x) = 4x^3 - x^2\) (pure numbers). Find \(f''(x)\) and then \(f''(1)\). Is the graph concave up or down at \(x = 1\)?

Show answer

\(f'(x) = 12x^2 - 2x\) and \(f''(x) = 24x - 2\). At \(x = 1\): \(24 - 2 = 22 \gt 0\), so concave up.

Exercise 3.3. A ball is thrown up at 30 m/s, so its height is \(z(t) = 30t - 5t^2\) metres (same toy gravity). When is it highest, how do you know it is a maximum, and how high does it go?

Show answer

\(z'(t) = 30 - 10t = 0\) at \(t = 3\) s. \(z''(t) = -10\ \text{m/s}^2 \lt 0\), so it is a maximum. \(z(3) = 90 - 45 = 45\) m.

Exercise 3.4. Let \(f(x) = x^2 - 6x + 1\). Find its critical point, classify it with the second-derivative test, and give \(f\) there.

Show answer

\(f'(x) = 2x - 6 = 0\) at \(x = 3\). \(f''(x) = 2 \gt 0\): a minimum. \(f(3) = 9 - 18 + 1 = -8\).

Exercise 3.5. Compute the second difference of \(f(x) = x^2\) at \(x = 3\) with \(\Delta x = 0.5\).

Show answer

\(f(3.5) = 12.25\), \(f(3) = 9\), \(f(2.5) = 6.25\). \(\dfrac{12.25 - 18 + 6.25}{0.25} = \dfrac{0.5}{0.25} = 2\). Exactly \(f''(x) = 2\), as Section 3.6 predicts for any parabola.

Exercise 3.6. Compute the second difference of \(e^x\) at 0 with \(\Delta x = 0.1\), using \(e^{0.1} \approx 1.105171\) and \(e^{-0.1} \approx 0.904837\). How far is it from the true \(f''(0)\)?

Show answer

\(\dfrac{1.105171 - 2 + 0.904837}{0.01} = \dfrac{0.010008}{0.01} = 1.0008\) (with more digits, 1.000834). The true value is \(e^0 = 1\), so the error is about 0.0008. With \(\Delta x = 0.01\) it would be about 100 times smaller.

Exercise 3.7. Where does \(f(x) = x^3 - 6x^2 + 9x\) have an inflection point?

Show answer

\(f'(x) = 3x^2 - 12x + 9\) and \(f''(x) = 6x - 12\). This is 0 at \(x = 2\), negative to the left and positive to the right, so it changes sign: an inflection point at \(x = 2\).

Exercise 3.8. Express the car's acceleration, 2 m/s², in metres per minute per minute.

Show answer

1 s = 1/60 min, so 1/s² = 3600/min². \(2\ \text{m/s}^2 = 2 \times 3600\ \text{m/min}^2 = 7200\ \text{m/min}^2\). The input unit grew 60 times; the second derivative grew \(60^2\) times.

Exercise 3.9. How far above its tangent line at \(t = 5\) s is the car's curve at \(t = 5.2\) s? Use the second derivative, then check directly.

Show answer

Gap \(= \tfrac12 s''\,\Delta t^2 = \tfrac12 \cdot 2\ \text{m/s}^2 \cdot (0.2\ \text{s})^2 = 0.04\) m. Direct: \(s(5.2) = 27.04\) m, and the tangent gives \(25 + 10 \cdot 0.2 = 27\) m. Gap 0.04 m.

Exercise 3.10. On the stop-to-stop trip, \(q(t) = 1.5t^2 - 0.1t^3\), at what time is the acceleration zero, and what is the speed then? Why is that the top speed?

Show answer

\(q''(t) = 3 - 0.6t = 0\) at \(t = 5\) s. Speed \(q'(5) = 15 - 7.5 = 7.5\) m/s. Before 5 s the acceleration is positive (speed rising); after, negative (speed falling). So the speed rises up to 5 s and falls after it: 5 s is a critical point of the speed \(q'\), and it is a maximum. In the language of Section 3.8, \(t = 5\) s is the inflection point of the position.
