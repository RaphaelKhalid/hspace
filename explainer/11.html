Level 11: Hessian-vector products

The full Hessian of a language model is far too big to write down. Its product with one direction is cheap, exact, and is all H-space ever computes.

By the end of this level you can
  • count the entries of a Hessian and say why the full matrix is out of reach at \(d = 5{,}120\);
  • say what \(H\mathbf{v}\) means in words, with units: how the gradient changes when you move along \(\mathbf{v}\);
  • estimate \(H\mathbf{v}\) by finite differences, and explain why the error first falls and then rises as the step shrinks;
  • compute \(H\mathbf{v}\) exactly by differentiating the backward pass (double backward), by hand on the tiny network;
  • check an HVP against finite differences with a relative error;
  • explain fp64, fp32 and bf16, and why the project stores weights in bf16 but computes in fp32.

Prerequisites: Level 4 (Taylor approximation), Level 9 (the Hessian, the quadratic form \(\mathbf{v}^\top H\mathbf{v}\), eigenvectors) and Level 10 (the chain rule, the tiny network, forward and backward passes).

11.1 The size problem: count the entries

At Level 9 the Hessian was a 2×2 matrix. For a function of \(d\) inputs it is \(d\times d\): one row and one column per input. Let us count for the model in this project.

  • Qwen3.6-27B has \(d = 5{,}120\) numbers per token position in its residual stream (theory-v2 §0).
  • One position's Hessian block has \(d^2 = 5{,}120 \times 5{,}120 = 26{,}214{,}400\) entries. Stored in fp32 (4 bytes each, 11.6) that is \(104{,}857{,}600\) bytes, about 105 MB. Symmetry (\(H_{jk} = H_{kj}\)) saves only half: \(d(d+1)/2 = 13{,}109{,}760\) distinct entries.
  • The 27B run perturbs \(N = 111\) valid positions per window (theory-v2 §6A: windows of 128 tokens, of which 111 are valid positions). The full Hessian over all of them, including how a nudge at one position changes the gradient at another, is \((N d)\times(N d) = 568{,}320 \times 568{,}320\). That is \(322{,}987{,}622{,}400 \approx 3.2\times10^{11}\) entries, about 1.3 TB in fp32. Per context, per layer.

Storage is the smaller problem. Computing is the bigger one. The only general way to get a Hessian column is to ask “how does the gradient change if I nudge input \(j\)?”: one Hessian-vector product with \(\mathbf{v}\) equal to the \(j\)-th unit vector. Filling the full matrix takes \(N d = 568{,}320\) such products per context. The run in theory-v2 §6A used \(256\) probes \(\times\ 4\) windows \(= 1{,}024\) sequence HVPs per layer, in total.

The way out Never build \(H\). Only ever ask for \(H\mathbf{v}\) for a few well-chosen directions \(\mathbf{v}\). One HVP returns \(N d\) numbers (568,320 here, about 2.3 MB), and costs a few backward passes, whatever \(d\) is.

11.2 What \(H\mathbf{v}\) means: how the gradient changes along \(\mathbf{v}\)

Recall that the Hessian is the Jacobian of the gradient (Level 9). The gradient \(\nabla f\) is a list of slopes. Its Jacobian \(H\) says how each slope changes when each input changes. Multiplying by \(\mathbf{v}\) is a Jacobian-vector product (Level 10): it says how all the slopes change together when the inputs move along \(\mathbf{v}\).

Definition $$ H\mathbf{v} = \lim_{\varepsilon\to0}\frac{\nabla f(\mathbf{x} + \varepsilon\mathbf{v}) - \nabla f(\mathbf{x})}{\varepsilon}, \qquad (H\mathbf{v})_j = \sum_k H_{jk}\,v_k . $$ Entry \(j\) of \(H\mathbf{v}\) is the rate at which the \(j\)-th slope \(\partial f/\partial x_j\) changes, per unit of movement along \(\mathbf{v}\). It has the units of the gradient.

Intuition pump: pressing on a mattress

Lie a marble on a mattress. The tilt of the surface under it, in every direction, is the gradient. Now press down a little on one side, along some direction \(\mathbf{v}\). The tilt changes, not only in the direction you pressed but in every direction: a well-stitched mattress couples neighbouring springs. \(H\mathbf{v}\) is the full list of tilt changes produced by that one press. You learn a whole column's worth of information (one response pattern) without testing the springs one pair at a time, which is what writing out \(H\) would mean.

Worked example with units: the farm

The farm of Level 5 has yield \(Y\) in bushels, with water in buckets and fertiliser in bags. Its Hessian (Level 9) is constant:

$$ H = \begin{pmatrix} -0.5 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & -1 \end{pmatrix} \quad \begin{pmatrix} \text{bushels/bucket}^2 & \text{bushels/(bucket}\cdot\text{bag)} \\ \text{bushels/(bucket}\cdot\text{bag)} & \text{bushels/bag}^2 \end{pmatrix}. $$

Move along \(\mathbf{v} = (1\ \text{bucket},\ 1\ \text{bag})\). Row by row:

  • first entry: \((-0.5\ \tfrac{\text{bushels}}{\text{bucket}^2})(1\ \text{bucket}) + (0.5\ \tfrac{\text{bushels}}{\text{bucket}\cdot\text{bag}})(1\ \text{bag}) = -0.5 + 0.5 = 0\) bushels per bucket;
  • second entry: \((0.5\ \tfrac{\text{bushels}}{\text{bucket}\cdot\text{bag}})(1\ \text{bucket}) + (-1\ \tfrac{\text{bushels}}{\text{bag}^2})(1\ \text{bag}) = 0.5 - 1 = -0.5\) bushels per bag.

Each unit cancels to leave the unit of a gradient entry. Mechanics check. At (4 buckets, 2 bags) the gradient is (1 bushel per bucket, 3 bushels per bag). At (5, 3), one step along \(\mathbf{v}\) later, it is (1, 2.5). The change is (0, −0.5): exactly \(H\mathbf{v}\), because the farm is quadratic. In words: adding a bucket and a bag together leaves the value of the next bucket unchanged (the interaction +0.5 cancels the diminishing return −0.5), and makes the next bag worth half a bushel less.

Dot it with \(\mathbf{v}\) once more and you get the curvature along \(\mathbf{v}\) from Level 9: \(\mathbf{v}^\top H\mathbf{v} = 1\times0 + 1\times(-0.5) = -0.5\) bushels.

The tiny network

For the tiny network of Level 10 at \(\mathbf{x} = (1, 2)\), with \(\mathbf{v} = (1, 0.5)\), we will find in 11.5 that \(H\mathbf{v} = (0.2528, -0.4871)\). So walking a small distance \(t\) along \(\mathbf{v}\) changes \(\partial y/\partial x_1\) by about \(+0.2528\,t\) and \(\partial y/\partial x_2\) by about \(-0.4871\,t\).

11.3 The finite-difference HVP

The definition suggests an estimate: compute two gradients by backpropagation and subtract. Let \(\varepsilon\) be a small step size.

$$ \text{forward difference:}\quad H\mathbf{v} \approx \frac{\nabla f(\mathbf{x} + \varepsilon\mathbf{v}) - \nabla f(\mathbf{x})}{\varepsilon}, \qquad \text{central difference:}\quad H\mathbf{v} \approx \frac{\nabla f(\mathbf{x} + \varepsilon\mathbf{v}) - \nabla f(\mathbf{x} - \varepsilon\mathbf{v})}{2\varepsilon}. $$

Each costs two gradients: cheap. But how accurate is it?

Worked example on the tiny network

Take \(\varepsilon = 0.01\). The shifted points are \(\mathbf{x} \pm 0.01\,\mathbf{v} = (1.01, 2.005)\) and \((0.99, 1.995)\). Backpropagation (Level 10) at each gives

$$ \nabla y(1.01, 2.005) = (-0.128050,\ -1.436215), \qquad \nabla y(0.99, 1.995) = (-0.133106,\ -1.426472), $$

and at the base point \(\nabla y(1, 2) = (-0.130589,\ -1.431352)\).

  • Forward: \(\big((-0.128050) - (-0.130589),\ (-1.436215) - (-1.431352)\big)/0.01 = (0.2539,\ -0.4862)\).
  • Central: \(\big((-0.128050) - (-0.133106),\ (-1.436215) - (-1.426472)\big)/0.02 = (0.25282,\ -0.48712)\).

The exact answer (11.5) is \((0.25282, -0.48712)\). The central difference is right to five digits; the forward one only to about two.

Why central beats forward: Taylor along the line

Follow one entry of the gradient as you walk along \(\mathbf{v}\). Call it \(k(t)\) = that entry at the point \(\mathbf{x} + t\mathbf{v}\). Then the matching entry of \(H\mathbf{v}\) is \(k'(0)\). Taylor's formula from Level 4 gives

$$ k(\varepsilon) = k(0) + \varepsilon\,k'(0) + \tfrac12\varepsilon^2 k''(0) + \tfrac16\varepsilon^3 k'''(0) + O(\varepsilon^4), $$ $$ k(-\varepsilon) = k(0) - \varepsilon\,k'(0) + \tfrac12\varepsilon^2 k''(0) - \tfrac16\varepsilon^3 k'''(0) + O(\varepsilon^4). $$
  • Forward: subtract \(k(0)\) from the first line and divide by \(\varepsilon\): \(\dfrac{k(\varepsilon)-k(0)}{\varepsilon} = k'(0) + \tfrac12\varepsilon\,k''(0) + O(\varepsilon^2)\). The error shrinks like \(\varepsilon\).
  • Central: subtract the second line from the first. The even terms cancel exactly: \(k(\varepsilon) - k(-\varepsilon) = 2\varepsilon k'(0) + \tfrac13\varepsilon^3k'''(0) + O(\varepsilon^5)\). Divide by \(2\varepsilon\): \(k'(0) + \tfrac16\varepsilon^2k'''(0) + O(\varepsilon^4)\). The error shrinks like \(\varepsilon^2\).

This error, from cutting the Taylor series short, is called truncation error. It says: make \(\varepsilon\) as small as possible. The next section explains why you cannot.

11.4 Rounding: why the step cannot be too small

Computers store real numbers with a fixed number of significant binary digits (floating point; details in 11.8). Every stored result is rounded. The relative size of that rounding is at most the unit roundoff \(u\): about \(6\times10^{-8}\) in fp32 and \(1.1\times10^{-16}\) in fp64.

Real-world example. Weigh a lorry with a 2-tonne load, then weigh it after a pigeon lands on the roof, on a weighbridge that reads to the nearest 10 kg. The difference is either 0 kg or 10 kg. Dividing by the pigeon's mass gives nonsense. Subtracting two nearly equal measurements throws away almost all their significant digits. This is called catastrophic cancellation.

The finite-difference HVP does exactly that. When \(\varepsilon\) is tiny, \(k(\varepsilon)\) and \(k(-\varepsilon)\) agree in almost every digit. Each carries a rounding error of about \(u\,|k|\). Their difference keeps that absolute error, and dividing by \(2\varepsilon\) blows it up:

$$ \text{rounding error} \approx \frac{u\,|k|}{\varepsilon}, \qquad \text{truncation error (central)} \approx \frac{\varepsilon^2}{6}\,|k'''(0)| . $$

One error falls as \(\varepsilon\) shrinks and the other rises. Their sum is smallest where the two are about equal, which happens when \(\varepsilon^3\) is about \(u\) (times a ratio of derivatives that is often near 1). So the best step is roughly \(u^{1/3}\), and the best relative error roughly \(u^{2/3}\):

  • fp64: best \(\varepsilon \approx (1.1\times10^{-16})^{1/3} \approx 5\times10^{-6}\); best error \(\approx 2\times10^{-11}\).
  • fp32: best \(\varepsilon \approx (6\times10^{-8})^{1/3} \approx 4\times10^{-3}\); best error \(\approx 1.5\times10^{-5}\).

These are order-of-magnitude guides, not exact values: the constants depend on the function.

The trap “Smaller step, more accurate” is only true down to the bottom of the valley. Past it, a smaller \(\varepsilon\) makes the estimate worse, and in fp32 at \(\varepsilon \approx 10^{-7}\) it is pure noise (relative error near 1). A finite-difference HVP is a check, not a measurement tool.

11.5 The exact HVP: differentiate the backward pass

There is a way to get \(H\mathbf{v}\) with no step size at all, exact up to ordinary rounding, at the cost of a few gradients. The idea (Pearlmutter 1994, cited in theory-v2 §1 among the standard references for Hessian backpropagation) is simple to say: the backward pass is itself a chain of ordinary formulas, so it can be differentiated with the chain rule like anything else. Differentiating the gradient along \(\mathbf{v}\) is, by definition, \(H\mathbf{v}\).

One more derivative we need: \(\operatorname{silu}''\)

From Level 10, \(\sigma' = \sigma(1-\sigma)\) and \(\operatorname{silu}'(z) = \sigma(z) + z\,\sigma'(z)\).

  1. Differentiate \(\sigma' = \sigma - \sigma^2\): \(\sigma'' = \sigma' - 2\sigma\sigma' = \sigma'(1 - 2\sigma)\).
  2. Differentiate \(\operatorname{silu}' = \sigma + z\sigma'\) with the product rule on the second term: \(\operatorname{silu}'' = \sigma' + (1\cdot\sigma' + z\,\sigma'') = 2\sigma' + z\,\sigma''\).
  3. Substitute step 1: \(\operatorname{silu}''(z) = \sigma'(z)\big(2 + z(1 - 2\sigma(z))\big) = \sigma(1-\sigma)\big(2 + z(1-2\sigma)\big)\).
  4. At \(z_1 = -0.5\): \(\sigma = 0.3775\), \(\sigma' = 0.3775\times0.6225 = 0.2350\), \(1 - 2\sigma = 0.2449\), so \(\operatorname{silu}'' = 0.2350\times(2 - 0.1225) = 0.2350\times1.8775 = 0.4412\).
  5. At \(z_2 = 1.5\): \(\sigma = 0.8176\), \(\sigma' = 0.1491\), \(1 - 2\sigma = -0.6352\), so \(\operatorname{silu}'' = 0.1491\times(2 - 0.9527) = 0.1491\times1.0473 = 0.1562\).

Forward-over-reverse, line by line

Write the forward and backward passes of Level 10 in the left column. In the right column, take the tangent value of each line along \(\mathbf{v}\): its rate of change per unit of movement along \(\mathbf{v}\), by the chain rule and the product rule.

The product rule in the fourth row is where curvature enters. \(\bar{\mathbf{z}}\) is a product of the adjoint and the slope \(\operatorname{silu}'(\mathbf{z})\). The slope changes along \(\mathbf{v}\) at the rate \(\operatorname{silu}''(\mathbf{z})\odot\dot{\mathbf{z}}\). The linear lines (\(W\), \(W^\top\), \(\mathbf{c}\)) just pass tangents through; they create no curvature.

The full Hessian of the tiny network, for comparison

Because the last row holds for every \(\mathbf{v}\), we can read off the matrix:

$$ H\mathbf{v} = W^\top\,\operatorname{diag}\big(\mathbf{c}\odot\operatorname{silu}''(\mathbf{z})\big)\,W\,\mathbf{v} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad H = W^\top\,\operatorname{diag}\big(c_1\operatorname{silu}''(z_1),\ c_2\operatorname{silu}''(z_2)\big)\,W . $$

The diagonal entries are \(1.5\times0.4412 = 0.6618\) and \(-1\times0.1562 = -0.1562\). Entry by entry, \(H_{jk} = 0.6618\,W_{1j}W_{1k} - 0.1562\,W_{2j}W_{2k}\):

$$ H_{11} = 0.6618(1)(1) - 0.1562(0.5)(0.5) = 0.6228, \quad H_{12} = 0.6618(1)(-1) - 0.1562(0.5)(1) = -0.7399, $$ $$ H_{22} = 0.6618(-1)(-1) - 0.1562(1)(1) = 0.5056 . $$

Check: \(H\mathbf{v} = (0.6228 - 0.7399\times0.5,\ -0.7399 + 0.5056\times0.5) = (0.2528, -0.4871)\). Same. Its eigenvalues are 1.306 and −0.178 (a saddle, Level 9), and \(\mathbf{v}^\top H\mathbf{v} = 0.2528 - 0.4871\times0.5 = 0.0093\): along this particular \(\mathbf{v}\) the two curvatures almost cancel.

Reverse-over-reverse: what hlens/hvp.py does

There is a second route to the same number. Define the scalar \(s(\mathbf{x}) = \nabla y(\mathbf{x})\cdot\mathbf{v}\): the slope along a fixed \(\mathbf{v}\). Its gradient is

$$ \frac{\partial s}{\partial x_k} = \sum_j \frac{\partial}{\partial x_k}\Big(\frac{\partial y}{\partial x_j}\Big)v_j = \sum_j H_{jk}\,v_j = \sum_j H_{kj}\,v_j = (H\mathbf{v})_k , $$

where the third step uses the symmetry \(H_{jk} = H_{kj}\) (Level 9). So: compute the gradient, dot it with \(\mathbf{v}\), and backpropagate a second time. That is what the project's code does. In hlens/hvp.py, the function grad_and_hvp runs the model from the layer-\(\ell\) residual stream to the output, computes the objective, takes its gradient with create_graph=True (which tells PyTorch to keep a record of how the gradient itself was computed, so that it can be differentiated), forms the dot product of that gradient with the tangent direction, and calls the gradient routine again. The file's docstring notes that this reverse-over-reverse result is the same exact quantity as forward-over-reverse.

Cost

Forward-over-reverse runs a forward pass, a backward pass, and the tangent of each. That is a small constant number of gradient-sized passes, independent of \(d\). theory-v2 §6 counts cost in “backward-equivalents”: it assumes \(c_{\text{HVP}} \approx 4\) and notes that PyTorch benchmarks put an HVP at 4 to 5 times a gradient (which gives \(c_{\text{HVP}} \approx 6\) to 7.5). Either way: a handful of passes for \(N d = 568{,}320\) numbers of exact second-order information.

11.6 The tiny network's HVP against its full Hessian

For a 2-input toy, building \(H\) is easy, so we can compare every route. In a real model only the HVP routes exist.

11.7 Checking an HVP: relative error

Exact HVP code can still have bugs. The standard check is to compare it with a central finite difference, using a size-free measure. For an estimate \(\mathbf{p}\) of a reference \(\mathbf{q}\):

$$ \text{relative error} = \frac{\lVert\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{q}\rVert}{\lVert\mathbf{q}\rVert}. $$

It is a pure number: “how big is the miss, compared with the thing itself”. Worked example: the forward difference of 11.3, \(\mathbf{p} = (0.25389, -0.48623)\), against \(\mathbf{q} = (0.25282, -0.48712)\): \(\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{q} = (0.00107, 0.00089)\), length \(\sqrt{0.00107^2 + 0.00089^2} = 0.00139\); \(\lVert\mathbf{q}\rVert = \sqrt{0.25282^2 + 0.48712^2} = 0.5488\). Relative error \(0.00139/0.5488 = 0.0025\).

What the project measured. theory-v2 §1 reports that the double-backward HVP on Qwen3.5-0.8B matches central finite differences with relative error \(1.3\times10^{-4}\) at layer 12 and \(1.7\times10^{-4}\) at layer 8 (tests/test_hvp.py, \(T = 48\)). The test file runs the fp32 model, tries four step sizes (3%, 1%, 0.3% and 0.1% of the typical residual length) and keeps the best, because of the valley in Figure 11.2. An error around \(10^{-4}\) is the kind of floor the fp32 trade-off leaves; it does not suggest a wrong HVP. A real bug (a missing term, a wrong sign) shows up as an error near 1.

11.8 Number formats: fp64, fp32, bf16

A floating-point number is stored as a sign, an exponent (which power of two) and a fraction (the significant digits, also called the mantissa). The more fraction bits, the finer the spacing between neighbouring numbers.

formatbits: sign / exponent / fractionbytesunit roundoff \(u\)about this many decimal digits
fp64 (“double”)1 / 11 / 528\(2^{-53} \approx 1.1\times10^{-16}\)16
fp32 (“single”)1 / 8 / 234\(2^{-24} \approx 6.0\times10^{-8}\)7
bf16 (“brain float”)1 / 8 / 72\(2^{-8} \approx 3.9\times10^{-3}\)2 to 3

Mechanics. Near 1, fp32 numbers are spaced \(2^{-23} \approx 1.2\times10^{-7}\) apart. bf16 numbers are spaced \(2^{-7} = 0.0078125\) apart. So 1.01 cannot be stored in bf16: the neighbours are 1.0 and 1.0078125, and it rounds to 1.0078125 (an error of 0.0022). π becomes 3.140625 in bf16 and 3.1415927 in fp32.

bf16 has the same 8 exponent bits as fp32, so it covers the same range of sizes (up to about \(3.4\times10^{38}\)); it just keeps far fewer digits. In fact a bf16 number is exactly the top 16 bits of an fp32 number. Appending 16 zero bits turns it into an fp32 number with exactly the same value. Going up (bf16 to fp32) loses nothing. Only going down rounds.

The bf16-stored, fp32-computed trick (hlens/bf16w.py)

The problem, in the words of the file's docstring, with the numbers it gives:

  • Qwen checkpoints are stored in bf16. So the bf16 weights are the model, and an fp32 copy of a weight is exact (previous paragraph).
  • HVPs need fp32 arithmetic for activations, gradients and tangents: in bf16, with \(u \approx 0.004\), second-order quantities would drown in rounding. theory-v2 §6 records that the HVPs ran in fp32 (without TF32, a reduced-precision mode some GPUs use for fp32 matrix products).
  • Storing the 27B model in fp32 doubles its memory to 108 GB (bf16w.py). In bf16 it is about 54 GB (theory-v2 §6). The run held the whole bf16 model on a 96 GB GPU (theory-v2 §6).
  • The naive fix, upcasting each weight inside the layer, does not help: autograd saves whatever a layer used, for the backward pass (the “keep the forward values” rule of Level 10), so it would save an fp32 copy of every weight: the same 108 GB.

The fix, in words. The file replaces every linear layer with a custom autograd function. Its forward pass takes fp32 inputs, makes a temporary fp32 copy of the bf16 weight matrix, multiplies, and saves only the bf16 weight for later. Its backward pass is the transposed map (multiply the incoming adjoint by the weight matrix: the “transposed Jacobian” of Level 10), written as a second custom function that also saves only the bf16 weight. The backward of that second function is the first function again, because the transpose of the transpose is the original map. So the pair can be differentiated as many times as needed, and double backward (the HVP) works. Memory stays at the bf16 weights plus one temporary fp32 copy of whichever matrix is in use. The file also casts every other parameter (norm gains and the like) to fp32, and keeps a faster all-bf16 mode for forward-only stages, with the comment that it must never be used for HVPs.

11.9 Why this matters for H-space

  1. Every H-space number is built from HVPs. The operators of Level 15 are estimated from many HVPs of the objective \(F_c\) with respect to the layer-\(\ell\) residual stream, never from a stored Hessian. theory-v2 §6 notes that the pass count of this estimate does not depend on \(d\).
  2. The probes are random directions. Run 1 (theory-v2 §6A) drew, per probe, one direction and one set of random signs over positions, and computed one HVP. Why random directions recover averages is the subject of Level 13 (Hutchinson's idea).
  3. Exactness is checked, not assumed. The double-backward HVP was checked against central differences (11.7). Level 12 adds a second check: the sum of per-sublayer pieces matches it to about \(10^{-6}\).
  4. Precision is a design choice. bf16 storage plus fp32 compute is what made exact HVPs through a 27B model fit in memory.

Exercise 11.4. One input: \(f(x) = x^3\), so the “gradient” is \(f'(x) = 3x^2\) and the exact HVP with \(v = 1\) is \(f''(1) = 6\). Estimate it at \(x = 1\) with the forward difference and \(\varepsilon = 0.1\). Then with the central difference. Why is the central one exact here?

Show answer

Forward: \((3(1.1)^2 - 3(1)^2)/0.1 = (3.63 - 3)/0.1 = 6.3\). Central: \((3(1.1)^2 - 3(0.9)^2)/0.2 = (3.63 - 2.43)/0.2 = 6.0\). The central error is \(\tfrac16\varepsilon^2 k'''\) where \(k = f' = 3x^2\); its third derivative is 0, so there is no truncation error at all.

Exercise 11.5. Estimate the rounding part of a finite-difference HVP's relative error in fp32 (\(u = 6\times10^{-8}\)), for a gradient entry of size about 1, with \(\varepsilon = 10^{-6}\). Is that step a good choice?

\(u|k|/\varepsilon = 6\times10^{-8}/10^{-6} = 0.06\): a 6% error from rounding alone. No: it is far left of the valley in Figure 11.2. A step near \(10^{-2}\) to \(10^{-3}\) is much better in fp32.

Exercise 11.10. Reverse-over-reverse computes the gradient of \(s(\mathbf{x}) = \nabla f(\mathbf{x})\cdot\mathbf{v}\). Show that, without symmetry, this would give \(H^\top\mathbf{v}\) rather than \(H\mathbf{v}\). Why is that never a problem for a Hessian of a smooth function?

Show answer

\(\partial s/\partial x_k = \sum_j (\partial^2 f/\partial x_k\partial x_j)\,v_j\). The matrix being summed over its first index \(j\) is \(H_{jk}\) read along its column, so the result is \(\sum_j H_{jk}v_j = (H^\top\mathbf{v})_k\). For a function with continuous second derivatives the mixed partials are equal (Level 6), so \(H^\top = H\) and the result is \(H\mathbf{v}\).
