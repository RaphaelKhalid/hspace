Level 7: Vectors, matrices and the determinant

A vector is a list of numbers you can draw as an arrow. A matrix is a machine that moves arrows. The determinant says how much that machine stretches area.

By the end of this level you can
  • add, scale and measure vectors, and compute a dot product and the angle it hides;
  • apply a matrix to a vector in two ways (row by row, and column by column) and say the unit of every entry;
  • multiply matrices and explain why the order matters;
  • compute a 2×2 determinant, explain its sign, and use it to invert a matrix;
  • say what a basis, a span, a subspace and the rank are, with a numerical example of each;
  • project a vector onto a line or a plane, and read an outer product as “read along \(\mathbf{b}\), write along \(\mathbf{a}\)”.

Prerequisites: Level 0 (units and rates), Level 1 (functions and coordinates), and the gradient from Level 5, which was already a vector.

7.1 Vectors: arrows and lists

Stand on a street corner. Walk 3 blocks east, then 4 blocks north. Where are you? You can say it two ways.

  • As a list: “(3, 4)”, meaning 3 east and 4 north. Order matters: (4, 3) is a different place.
  • As an arrow: a straight arrow from the corner to where you ended up.

Both are the same object. It is called a vector. Let \(\mathbf{v}\) be this walk. Then \(\mathbf{v} = (3, 4)\), and each number in the list is a component: \(v_1 = 3\) blocks east and \(v_2 = 4\) blocks north. The number of components is the dimension, written \(d\). Here \(d = 2\). The set of all such pairs of real numbers is written \(\mathbb{R}^2\), “R two”.

A vector is not only a direction on a map. Any fixed-order list of numbers is one. A farm plan from Level 5, “4 buckets of water and 2 bags of fertiliser”, is the vector \((4, 2)\) in \(\mathbb{R}^2\). The farm gradient at that plan from Level 5, \(\nabla Y = (1, 3)\), was a vector too. A recipe for a cake (grams of flour, sugar, butter, eggs) is a vector in \(\mathbb{R}^4\).

Intuition pump: the arrow is portable An arrow means “this much east, this much north”. It does not care where it starts. The walk (3, 4) from your house and the walk (3, 4) from the station are the same vector. We usually draw it starting at the origin \(\mathbf{0} = (0, 0)\) so we can name it by where its tip lands.

7.2 Adding, scaling and length

Adding: do one walk, then the other

Let \(\mathbf{a} = (3, 1)\) and \(\mathbf{b} = (1, 2)\). Walking \(\mathbf{a}\) then \(\mathbf{b}\) puts you at

$$ \mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b} = (3 + 1,\ 1 + 2) = (4, 3). $$

Add matching components. Nothing else. As arrows, put the tail of \(\mathbf{b}\) on the tip of \(\mathbf{a}\) and draw from the start to the final tip. Walking \(\mathbf{b}\) first and then \(\mathbf{a}\) ends at the same place, so the two routes make a parallelogram (you will see it in Figure 7.1).

Mechanics. Change only \(b_2\) from 2 to 3. Then \(\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b} = (4, 4)\). Only the second component of the sum moved, and it moved by exactly the same amount, 1. Components never leak into each other when you add.

Scaling: stretch by a number

Let \(k\) be an ordinary number. In this context a single number is called a scalar. Then

$$ k\mathbf{v} = (k v_1,\ k v_2). \qquad 2\,(3, 1) = (6, 2), \qquad -1\,(3, 1) = (-3, -1), \qquad 0.5\,(3,1) = (1.5, 0.5). $$

A positive \(k\) stretches or shrinks the arrow along its own line. A negative \(k\) also flips it round. Adding scaled vectors, like \(2\mathbf{a} + 3\mathbf{b}\), is called a linear combination. Almost everything below is built from linear combinations.

Length: Pythagoras

The walk (3, 4) has a straight-line length you can find with Pythagoras' theorem: the two legs are 3 and 4, so the diagonal is \(\sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5\) blocks. We write this as the norm

$$ \lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert = \sqrt{v_1^2 + v_2^2}, \qquad \lVert (3, 4) \rVert = 5. $$

In 3-D, with \(\mathbf{v} = (v_1, v_2, v_3)\), you apply Pythagoras twice and get \(\sqrt{v_1^2 + v_2^2 + v_3^2}\). In \(d\) dimensions you keep going: square every component, add, take the square root.

Mechanics. Scale \(\mathbf{v}\) by \(k\): each square gets multiplied by \(k^2\), so \(\lVert k\mathbf{v}\rVert = \lvert k\rvert\,\lVert \mathbf{v}\rVert\). Doubling the walk doubles its length: \(\lVert (6, 8) \rVert = 10\).

Unit vectors: pure direction

Divide a vector by its own length and you get an arrow of length exactly 1 pointing the same way. That is a unit vector, written with a hat:

$$ \hat{\mathbf{v}} = \frac{\mathbf{v}}{\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert} = \frac{(3, 4)}{5} = (0.6,\ 0.8), \qquad \lVert (0.6, 0.8) \rVert = \sqrt{0.36 + 0.64} = 1. $$

A unit vector carries only a direction. We use it whenever we want to ask “how much in this direction?” without the answer depending on how long the probe arrow is.

Units warning: length can be meaningless The farm plan \((4\ \text{buckets}, 2\ \text{bags})\) has components with different units. Its “length” \(\sqrt{4^2 + 2^2}\) adds buckets² to bags². That number changes if you measure water in litres instead (\(\sqrt{40^2 + 2^2}\)). Length and angle only mean something once you decide how big one bucket is compared with one bag. This choice comes back at Level 9 and is the whole point of whitening at Level 15.

7.3 The dot product and the angle

Real-world example: the till receipt

You buy 4 apples and 3 pears. Apples cost 0.5 $ each and pears 0.8 $ each. Put the quantities in one vector and the prices in another:

$$ \mathbf{q} = (4\ \text{apples},\ 3\ \text{pears}), \qquad \mathbf{p} = (0.5\ \$/\text{apple},\ 0.8\ \$/\text{pear}). $$

The total bill multiplies matching components and adds:

$$ \mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{p} = 4\ \text{apples} \times 0.5\ \tfrac{\$}{\text{apple}} + 3\ \text{pears} \times 0.8\ \tfrac{\$}{\text{pear}} = 2\ \$ + 2.4\ \$ = 4.4\ \$. $$

That operation is the dot product. In general

$$ \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 + \dots + a_d b_d. $$

Notice the units. Each product (apples × $/apple) came out in dollars, so they could be added. A dot product only makes sense when every product \(a_i b_i\) has the same unit.

Mechanics. Buy one more pear: \(q_2\) goes from 3 to 4 and the bill rises by exactly \(p_2 = 0.8\) $. Raise the apple price by 0.1 $: the bill rises by \(q_1 \times 0.1 = 0.4\) $. Each component's change is weighted by the matching component of the other vector.

The hidden angle

Now a geometric fact. For two arrows \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\) with angle \(\theta\) between them,

$$ \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = \lVert \mathbf{a} \rVert\, \lVert \mathbf{b} \rVert \cos\theta. $$

Here \(\cos\theta\) is the cosine of the angle: 1 when the arrows point the same way, 0 when they are at right angles, −1 when they point opposite ways. Here is why the formula holds, step by step.

  1. Expand the squared length of the third side of the triangle, \(\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b}\), in components: $$ \lVert \mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b}\rVert^2 = (a_1 - b_1)^2 + (a_2 - b_2)^2 = a_1^2 - 2a_1b_1 + b_1^2 + a_2^2 - 2a_2b_2 + b_2^2. $$
  2. Regroup: \(= (a_1^2 + a_2^2) + (b_1^2 + b_2^2) - 2(a_1b_1 + a_2b_2) = \lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert^2 + \lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert^2 - 2\,\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}.\)
  3. School geometry (the law of cosines) says the same side satisfies \(\lVert \mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b}\rVert^2 = \lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert^2 + \lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert^2 - 2\lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert\cos\theta\).
  4. The two right-hand sides are equal. Cancel the matching terms: \(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = \lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert\cos\theta\).

Worked example

Let \(\mathbf{a} = (3, 1)\) and \(\mathbf{b} = (1, 2)\).

$$ \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = 3\times1 + 1\times2 = 5, \quad \lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert = \sqrt{10} \approx 3.1623, \quad \lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert = \sqrt{5} \approx 2.2361. $$ $$ \cos\theta = \frac{5}{3.1623 \times 2.2361} = \frac{5}{7.0711} = 0.7071 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \theta = 45^\circ. $$

Two arrows are orthogonal (perpendicular) exactly when their dot product is 0, because \(\cos 90^\circ = 0\). Example: \((2, 1)\cdot(-1, 2) = -2 + 2 = 0\).

Intuition pump: the dot product is a vote of agreement Think of \(\mathbf{a}\) as a wind and \(\mathbf{b}\) as the way you are cycling. The dot product is how much the wind helps you, times how far you go. Tailwind: large and positive. Crosswind: zero help. Headwind: negative. The size of each arrow scales the answer; the angle decides its sign.

7.4 Matrices as machines

Real-world example: the bakery

A bakery bakes loaves and cakes. One loaf needs 0.5 kg of flour and no eggs. One cake needs 0.3 kg of flour and 4 eggs. Write this as a grid, one row per ingredient and one column per item baked:

$$ R = \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 & 0.3 \\ 0 & 4 \end{pmatrix} \qquad \begin{array}{l} \leftarrow \text{flour (kg)} \\ \leftarrow \text{eggs} \end{array} $$

with column 1 = per loaf and column 2 = per cake. A grid of numbers like this is a matrix. Write \(R_{ij}\) for the entry in row \(i\) and column \(j\). Each entry has a unit: output unit per input unit.

EntryValueUnitMeaning
\(R_{11}\)0.5kg flour per loafflour used by one more loaf
\(R_{12}\)0.3kg flour per cakeflour used by one more cake
\(R_{21}\)0eggs per loafloaves use no eggs
\(R_{22}\)4eggs per cakeeggs used by one more cake

This is exactly the shape a Jacobian will have at Level 8: rows are outputs, columns are inputs, and each entry is “output per input”.

Now an order comes in: \(\mathbf{x} = (10\ \text{loaves}, 3\ \text{cakes})\). The shopping list \(\mathbf{y} = R\mathbf{x}\) can be computed two ways. Both give the same answer, and both are worth owning.

Row view: each output is a dot product

Output \(i\) is row \(i\) of the matrix dotted with the input:

$$ y_1 = (0.5, 0.3)\cdot(10, 3) = 0.5\times10 + 0.3\times3 = 5 + 0.9 = 5.9\ \text{kg flour}, $$ $$ y_2 = (0, 4)\cdot(10, 3) = 0\times10 + 4\times3 = 12\ \text{eggs}. $$

Units check: (kg/loaf) × loaves + (kg/cake) × cakes = kg + kg. Each row is a till receipt from 7.3.

Column view: add up scaled columns

Column \(j\) says what one unit of input \(j\) costs in every output. So the output is a linear combination of the columns, weighted by the input:

$$ R\mathbf{x} = 10 \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + 3 \begin{pmatrix} 0.3 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 0.9 \\ 12 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 5.9 \\ 12 \end{pmatrix}. $$

The row view asks “how is each output made?”. The column view asks “where does each input send me?”. In general, for a matrix \(A\) with entries \(A_{ij}\),

$$ (A\mathbf{v})_i = \sum_j A_{ij}\, v_j = A_{i1}v_1 + A_{i2}v_2 + \dots $$

The symbol \(\sum_j\) means “add up over every column \(j\)”. This is the matrix-vector product.

Mechanics. Add one cake to the order: \(\mathbf{x}\) becomes \((10, 4)\). The output changes by exactly column 2, \((0.3, 4)\): the list becomes (6.2 kg, 16 eggs). Add one loaf instead: the output changes by column 1, \((0.5, 0)\). One more unit of input \(j\) always adds column \(j\).

A geometric example

Let \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\) and \(\mathbf{v} = (1, 2)\). Row view: \((2\times1 + 1\times2,\ 1\times1 + 3\times2) = (4, 7)\). Column view: \(1\times(2, 1) + 2\times(1, 3) = (2, 1) + (2, 6) = (4, 7)\). Same answer.

7.5 Linear maps keep grids straight

A matrix has two properties that make it special among machines. For any vectors \(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w}\) and any scalar \(k\):

$$ A(\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w}) = A\mathbf{v} + A\mathbf{w}, \qquad A(k\mathbf{v}) = k\,A\mathbf{v}. $$

The bakery shows why: two orders together need the sum of their two shopping lists, and twice the order needs twice the list. A machine with these two properties is called a linear map. Every linear map on \(\mathbb{R}^2\) is some 2×2 matrix, and every matrix is a linear map.

Three consequences you can see in a picture:

  1. The origin stays put: \(A\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}\).
  2. Grid lines stay straight, parallel and evenly spaced. The line of points \(\mathbf{v} + t\mathbf{w}\) (for every number \(t\)) goes to \(A\mathbf{v} + t\,A\mathbf{w}\), which is again a straight line, with equal steps of \(t\) giving equal steps.
  3. The columns tell you everything. Let \(\mathbf{e}_1 = (1, 0)\) and \(\mathbf{e}_2 = (0, 1)\) be the standard basis arrows. Then \(A\mathbf{e}_1\) is column 1 and \(A\mathbf{e}_2\) is column 2. Since any \(\mathbf{v} = v_1\mathbf{e}_1 + v_2\mathbf{e}_2\), knowing where the two basis arrows go fixes where everything goes.
Read a 2×2 matrix at a glance Column 1 is where the arrow \((1, 0)\) lands. Column 2 is where \((0, 1)\) lands. The unit square with corners \((0,0), (1,0), (1,1), (0,1)\) lands on the parallelogram built from those two columns. Figure 7.2 below shows this live.

Contrast with a non-linear machine, like squaring each component. It sends \((1, 0) \mapsto (1, 0)\) and \((2, 0) \mapsto (4, 0)\): equal input steps, unequal output steps. Grid lines get bent or bunched. At Level 8 we zoom in on such bent grids until they look straight again.

7.6 Composition, the identity and the transpose

Doing two machines in a row

Let \(Q = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\) (a quarter turn anticlockwise: it sends \((1,0)\) to \((0,1)\)) and \(D = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\) (stretch horizontally by 2). Apply \(Q\) first, then \(D\), to \(\mathbf{e}_1 = (1, 0)\):

$$ Q\mathbf{e}_1 = (0, 1), \qquad D(0, 1) = (2\times0 + 0\times1,\ 0\times0 + 1\times1) = (0, 1). $$

Now the other order, \(D\) first, then \(Q\):

$$ D\mathbf{e}_1 = (2, 0), \qquad Q(2, 0) = (0\times2 + (-1)\times0,\ 1\times2 + 0\times0) = (0, 2). $$

Different answers. Order matters. Doing \(B\) first and then \(A\) is itself one linear map, the composition, and its matrix is the product \(AB\) (read right to left, like \(A(B\mathbf{v})\)). Its entries are row-of-\(A\) dot column-of-\(B\):

$$ (AB)_{ij} = \sum_m A_{im} B_{mj}. $$

Here \(m\) is just a counter that runs over the middle index. Worked example with \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\) and \(B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\):

$$ AB = \begin{pmatrix} 2\cdot1 + 1\cdot0 & 2\cdot2 + 1\cdot3 \\ 1\cdot1 + 1\cdot0 & 1\cdot2 + 1\cdot3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 7 \\ 1 & 5 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad BA = \begin{pmatrix} 1\cdot2 + 2\cdot1 & 1\cdot1 + 2\cdot1 \\ 0\cdot2 + 3\cdot1 & 0\cdot1 + 3\cdot1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 3 \\ 3 & 3 \end{pmatrix}. $$

Units chain and cancel. In the bakery, suppose a matrix \(B\) turns customer orders into items baked (loaves per order, cakes per order). Then \(RB\) turns orders into ingredients: (kg per loaf) × (loaves per order) = kg per order. The middle unit cancels, as in Level 0. This is the chain rule in disguise; at Level 10 it becomes literal.

The identity

The identity matrix \(I = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\) does nothing: \(I\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}\), and \(AI = IA = A\). It is the matrix version of the number 1.

The transpose

The transpose \(A^\top\) swaps rows and columns: \((A^\top)_{ij} = A_{ji}\). For a vector, we treat \(\mathbf{v}\) as a column (a column vector), and \(\mathbf{v}^\top\) is the same numbers written as a row (a row vector). Then a row times a column is exactly the dot product:

$$ \mathbf{a}^\top\mathbf{b} = \begin{pmatrix} a_1 & a_2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} b_1 \\ b_2 \end{pmatrix} = a_1b_1 + a_2b_2 = \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}. $$

One rule you will need at Level 10: \((AB)^\top = B^\top A^\top\). Check with the matrices above: \((AB)^\top = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 7 & 5 \end{pmatrix}\), and \(B^\top A^\top = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 4+3 & 2+3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 7 & 5 \end{pmatrix}\). Equal. A matrix with \(A^\top = A\) is called symmetric; every Hessian at Level 9 is one.

7.7 The determinant: signed area scaling

A linear map sends the unit square (area 1) to a parallelogram. Every other shape gets its area multiplied by the same factor, because any shape can be tiled by tiny squares and each tiny square is scaled the same way. That factor, with a sign attached, is the determinant.

Deriving \(ad - bc\)

Take \(A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}\) with all four numbers positive and \(ad \gt bc\). Its columns are \((a, c)\) and \((b, d)\). The parallelogram they span sits inside a rectangle of width \(a + b\) and height \(c + d\). The rectangle's area minus the pieces around the parallelogram is:

$$ \underbrace{(a+b)(c+d)}_{\text{rectangle}} - \underbrace{2\cdot\tfrac12 ac}_{\text{two triangles}} - \underbrace{2\cdot\tfrac12 bd}_{\text{two triangles}} - \underbrace{2\,bc}_{\text{two small rectangles}} $$ $$ = ac + ad + bc + bd - ac - bd - 2bc = ad - bc. $$
The determinant of a 2×2 matrix $$ \det \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} = ad - bc. $$ Its size is the factor by which areas are multiplied. Its sign says whether orientation is kept (+) or mirrored (−). Zero means the plane is squashed onto a line or a point.

Units. The determinant multiplies an entry from each row and each column, so its unit is (product of output units) per (product of input units): output area per input area. For the bakery, \(\det R = 0.5\times4 - 0.3\times0 = 2\) (kg·eggs) per (loaf·cake).

The sign: orientation

Label the unit square's corners 1, 2, 3, 4 going anticlockwise. If \(\det A \gt 0\) the images of the corners still go anticlockwise. If \(\det A \lt 0\) they go clockwise: the map includes a mirror flip, like reading a page in a mirror. Swapping the two columns flips the sign: \(\det\begin{pmatrix} b & a \\ d & c\end{pmatrix} = bc - ad = -(ad - bc)\).

Mechanics: change one entry

Start at \(A = I\), so \(\det = 1\cdot1 - 0\cdot0 = 1\). Raise \(a\) to 2: \(\det = 2\). The square stretched sideways and doubled in area. Now raise \(b\) from 0 to 1 (a shear): \(\det = 2\cdot1 - 1\cdot0 = 2\), unchanged. A shear slides layers sideways like a deck of cards and never changes area. Now raise \(c\) to 2: \(\det = 2\cdot1 - 1\cdot2 = 0\). The columns \((2, 2)\) and \((1, 1)\) are parallel, and the square has collapsed onto a line.

Composition multiplies determinants

If \(B\) scales area by \(\det B\) and then \(A\) scales by \(\det A\), the total factor is the product: \(\det(AB) = \det A \cdot \det B\). Check with 7.6: \(\det A = 2\cdot1 - 1\cdot1 = 1\), \(\det B = 1\cdot3 - 2\cdot0 = 3\), and \(\det(AB) = 2\cdot5 - 7\cdot1 = 3 = 1\times3\).

In 3-D the determinant of a 3×3 matrix is the signed volume scaling of the unit cube, and the same story holds in any dimension.

7.8 The inverse: running the machine backwards

The bakery manager sees 5.9 kg of flour and 12 eggs gone. What was baked? We want a matrix \(R^{-1}\) with \(R^{-1}R = I\). For a 2×2 matrix the recipe is: swap \(a\) and \(d\), negate \(b\) and \(c\), divide by the determinant:

$$ A^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad - bc}\begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix}. $$

Check it by multiplying, every entry:

$$ \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} ad - bc & -ab + ba \\ cd - dc & -cb + da \end{pmatrix} = (ad - bc)\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}. $$

Dividing by \(ad - bc\) leaves \(I\). For the bakery, \(\det R = 2\), so

$$ R^{-1} = \frac12\begin{pmatrix} 4 & -0.3 \\ 0 & 0.5 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -0.15 \\ 0 & 0.25 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad R^{-1}\begin{pmatrix} 5.9 \\ 12 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2\times5.9 - 0.15\times12 \\ 0\times5.9 + 0.25\times12 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 10 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}. $$

10 loaves and 3 cakes, as ordered. The units of \(R^{-1}\) are flipped: \(R^{-1}_{22} = 0.25\) cakes per egg (each egg “accounts for” a quarter of a cake), and \(R^{-1}_{11} = 2\) loaves per kg of flour.

When there is no inverse If \(\det A = 0\) the formula divides by zero. Geometrically, the plane was squashed onto a line, so many inputs share one output and no machine can tell them apart. Such a matrix is singular. Example: \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}\) sends both \((2, 0)\) and \((0, 1)\) to \((2, 4)\).

7.9 Basis, span, subspace and rank

Basis and coordinates

The arrows \(\mathbf{e}_1, \mathbf{e}_2\) are not the only way to build every vector. Take \(\mathbf{f}_1 = (1, 1)\) and \(\mathbf{f}_2 = (1, -1)\). To write \(\mathbf{v} = (3, 1)\) as \(c_1\mathbf{f}_1 + c_2\mathbf{f}_2\), solve:

$$ c_1 + c_2 = 3, \quad c_1 - c_2 = 1 \quad\Rightarrow\quad 2c_1 = 4,\ c_1 = 2,\ c_2 = 1. $$

Check: \(2(1,1) + 1(1,-1) = (3, 1)\). A set of directions that can build every vector, each in exactly one way, is a basis. The recipe numbers \((c_1, c_2) = (2, 1)\) are the coordinates of \(\mathbf{v}\) in that basis. Same arrow, different description, like giving a street address versus GPS numbers.

Because \(\mathbf{f}_1\) and \(\mathbf{f}_2\) are perpendicular, there is a shortcut: \(c_1 = \mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{f}_1 / \lVert\mathbf{f}_1\rVert^2 = 4/2 = 2\), and \(c_2 = \mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{f}_2/\lVert\mathbf{f}_2\rVert^2 = 2/2 = 1\). If the basis arrows are perpendicular and of length 1 (an orthonormal basis), each coordinate is one dot product. Changing to a basis that suits the problem is the key move of Level 9.

Span and subspace

The span of some vectors is everything you can build from them by scaling and adding. The span of \((1, 2)\) alone is the line through the origin in that direction. The span of \((1, 0, 0)\) and \((0, 1, 0)\) is the flat floor (the \(xy\)-plane) inside 3-D. A line or plane through the origin, closed under adding and scaling, is a subspace. Lines and planes that miss the origin are not subspaces, because scaling by 0 must give \(\mathbf{0}\).

Rank: how many directions survive

The outputs \(A\mathbf{v}\) of a matrix are linear combinations of its columns (column view), so they fill the span of the columns. The dimension of that span is the rank. Example:

$$ M = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad M\mathbf{v} = v_1\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} + v_2\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix} = (v_1 + 2v_2)\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}. $$

Every output lies on one line, so \(M\) has rank 1. Its determinant is \(1\cdot4 - 2\cdot2 = 0\), as it must be. In 2×2, rank 2 means \(\det \ne 0\); rank 1 or 0 means \(\det = 0\). A 3×3 matrix whose third column is the sum of the first two, like \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\), has rank 2: all outputs lie in the floor plane.

Mechanics. Change one entry of \(M\): set \(M_{22} = 4.1\). The columns are now \((1, 2)\) and \((2, 4.1)\), not parallel. The determinant becomes \(4.1 - 4 = 0.1\) and the rank jumps to 2. Rank does not change smoothly; it jumps.

7.10 Projection onto a line and onto a subspace

At noon the sun is straight overhead, and your shadow on flat ground is your projection onto the ground. Projection keeps the part of a vector that lies along a line (or plane) and throws away the rest.

Onto a line

Let the line go through the origin in direction \(\mathbf{u}\), and let \(\hat{\mathbf{u}} = \mathbf{u}/\lVert\mathbf{u}\rVert\). The amount of \(\mathbf{v}\) along the line is the dot product \(\mathbf{v}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{u}}\) (a number). The projection is that much of \(\hat{\mathbf{u}}\):

$$ \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{u}}\mathbf{v} = (\mathbf{v}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{u}})\,\hat{\mathbf{u}}. $$

Worked example: \(\mathbf{v} = (3, 1)\), \(\mathbf{u} = (1, 1)\).

$$ \hat{\mathbf{u}} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, 1) \approx (0.7071, 0.7071), \qquad \mathbf{v}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{u}} = \tfrac{3 + 1}{\sqrt2} = \tfrac{4}{\sqrt 2} \approx 2.8284, $$ $$ \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{u}}\mathbf{v} = 2.8284 \times (0.7071, 0.7071) = (2, 2). $$

The residual is \(\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{v} - \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{u}}\mathbf{v} = (1, -1)\). It is perpendicular to the line: \((1, -1)\cdot(1, 1) = 0\). That is what makes this the closest point on the line to \(\mathbf{v}\), by Pythagoras: any other point on the line adds a second perpendicular leg and so is further away.

As a matrix. Write \((\mathbf{v}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{u}})\hat{\mathbf{u}} = \hat{\mathbf{u}}(\hat{\mathbf{u}}^\top\mathbf{v}) = (\hat{\mathbf{u}}\hat{\mathbf{u}}^\top)\mathbf{v}\). So the projection matrix is \(P = \hat{\mathbf{u}}\hat{\mathbf{u}}^\top\). For \(\mathbf{u} = (1,1)\), \(P = \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & 0.5 \end{pmatrix}\). Check: \(P(3, 1) = (1.5 + 0.5, 1.5 + 0.5) = (2, 2)\). Projecting twice changes nothing more, so \(PP = P\). Its rank is 1 and its determinant is \(0.25 - 0.25 = 0\).

Onto a subspace

To project onto a plane in 3-D spanned by two perpendicular unit vectors \(\mathbf{u}_1, \mathbf{u}_2\), project onto each and add:

$$ \operatorname{proj}\mathbf{v} = (\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{u}_1)\mathbf{u}_1 + (\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{u}_2)\mathbf{u}_2, \qquad P = \mathbf{u}_1\mathbf{u}_1^\top + \mathbf{u}_2\mathbf{u}_2^\top. $$

Worked example: \(\mathbf{u}_1 = (1, 0, 0)\), \(\mathbf{u}_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(0, 1, 1)\), \(\mathbf{v} = (1, 2, 3)\).

$$ \mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{u}_1 = 1, \qquad \mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{u}_2 = \tfrac{2 + 3}{\sqrt2} = \tfrac{5}{\sqrt2}, $$ $$ \operatorname{proj}\mathbf{v} = 1\cdot(1, 0, 0) + \tfrac{5}{\sqrt2}\cdot\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(0, 1, 1) = (1, 0, 0) + (0, 2.5, 2.5) = (1, 2.5, 2.5). $$

Residual \((1,2,3) - (1, 2.5, 2.5) = (0, -0.5, 0.5)\). Check it is perpendicular to both: \((0,-0.5,0.5)\cdot(1,0,0) = 0\) and \((0,-0.5,0.5)\cdot(0,1,1) = -0.5 + 0.5 = 0\). The shortcut “add the single projections” works only because \(\mathbf{u}_1 \perp \mathbf{u}_2\). With a tilted pair you would double-count.

7.11 The outer product: read along b, write along a

The dot product \(\mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{v}\) (row times column) gives one number. Flip the order to column times row and you get a whole matrix, the outer product:

$$ \mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^\top = \begin{pmatrix} a_1 \\ a_2 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} b_1 & b_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} a_1b_1 & a_1b_2 \\ a_2b_1 & a_2b_2 \end{pmatrix}. $$

What does this machine do to a vector \(\mathbf{v}\)? Group the brackets differently:

$$ (\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^\top)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{a}\,(\mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{v}) = s\,\mathbf{a}, \qquad s = \mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{v} = b_1v_1 + b_2v_2. $$
Read along b, write along a The outer product \(\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^\top\) reads one number from its input, \(s = \mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{v}\), and writes that many copies of \(\mathbf{a}\). Every output lies on the line through \(\mathbf{a}\), so the rank is 1. Every input perpendicular to \(\mathbf{b}\) gives \(s = 0\): those inputs are invisible to the machine (they form its null space).

Worked example: \(\mathbf{a} = (2, 1)\), \(\mathbf{b} = (1, 3)\), \(\mathbf{v} = (1, 1)\).

$$ \mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^\top = \begin{pmatrix} 2\cdot1 & 2\cdot3 \\ 1\cdot1 & 1\cdot3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 6 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad s = 1\cdot1 + 3\cdot1 = 4, \qquad s\,\mathbf{a} = (8, 4). $$

Check by the row view: \((2\cdot1 + 6\cdot1,\ 1\cdot1 + 3\cdot1) = (8, 4)\). Same. The determinant is \(2\cdot3 - 6\cdot1 = 0\), as for every outer product in 2-D.

Mechanics. Move \(\mathbf{v}\) to \((3, -1)\), which is perpendicular to \(\mathbf{b}\): \(s = 3 - 3 = 0\) and the output is \((0, 0)\). Move it to \((1, 2)\): \(s = 7\), output \((14, 7)\). The output only ever slides along \(\mathbf{a}\); its length is set by how much of \(\mathbf{b}\) the input contains.

Real-world example. A microphone and a loudspeaker. The microphone faces one way (\(\mathbf{b}\)) and turns the air pressure into one number. The loudspeaker faces another way (\(\mathbf{a}\)) and plays that number out. Sound arriving side-on to the microphone is never played. The projection matrix \(\hat{\mathbf{u}}\hat{\mathbf{u}}^\top\) from 7.10 is the special case where the microphone and loudspeaker face the same way, and the rank-1 matrix \(M\) of 7.9 is \(\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^\top\) with \(\mathbf{a} = (1, 2)\).

7.12 Why this matters for H-space

Everything on this page works the same with more components. A residual state of Qwen3.6-27B is a vector with \(d = 5{,}120\) components; for Qwen3.5-0.8B, \(d = 1{,}024\) (sizes from hlens/paper/theory-v2.md §0). A dot product of two such states is a sum of 5,120 products. A 5,120 × 5,120 matrix has \(5{,}120^2 = 26{,}214{,}400\) entries. Nothing new is needed; only the drawings stop being possible. Here is where each idea returns.

  1. Dot products as readouts. The J-lens at Level 14 decodes with \(\mathbf{u}_t^\top J_\ell\,\mathbf{h}\): a row vector (one per vocabulary token) dotted with a matrix-vector product. That is the row view of 7.4.
  2. Matrices as “output per input”. The J-lens \(J_\ell\) is a 5,120 × 5,120 matrix whose entry \((i, j)\) says how much final-state component \(i\) moves per unit of layer-\(\ell\) component \(j\). That is the bakery table, enlarged.
  3. Outer products as reads and writes. A SwiGLU neuron (Level 14) reads two numbers from the residual state along two directions, bends one of them through SiLU, and multiplies them. At Level 9 you will see that a plain product of two reads has Hessian \(\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^\top + \mathbf{b}\mathbf{a}^\top\): two outer products. theory-v2 §1 and §2 build the curvature of the whole model from such pieces.
  4. Subspaces and projection. H-space is a low-dimensional subspace (25 dimensions by default) of the 5,120-dimensional state space. Ablating it means projecting it out: keeping the residual \(\mathbf{r}\) of 7.10.
  5. Bases and units. The units warning in 7.2 is the reason Level 15 whitens: it picks a basis in which one step means one natural spread of the activations.
A high-dimensional surprise In 2-D, two arrows pointing in random directions often make a small angle. In 5,120-D they almost never do: for two random directions the cosine is typically about \(1/\sqrt{d} \approx 1/71.6 \approx 0.014\), so they are nearly orthogonal. This is why, at Level 13, a random 25-dimensional subspace overlaps a fixed one by only about \(25/d\).

Exercises

Exercise 7.1. Find \(\lVert (5, 12) \rVert\), then the unit vector in the same direction.

Show answer

\(\sqrt{5^2 + 12^2} = \sqrt{25 + 144} = \sqrt{169} = 13\). Unit vector: \((5/13, 12/13) \approx (0.3846, 0.9231)\). Check: \(0.3846^2 + 0.9231^2 = 0.1479 + 0.8521 = 1\).

Exercise 7.2. You buy 2 loaves at 1.5 $ per loaf, 5 eggs at 0.2 $ per egg and 3 kg of flour at 1.1 $ per kg. Write the purchase as a dot product and compute the bill. State the unit of each product before you add.

Show answer

\((2, 5, 3)\cdot(1.5, 0.2, 1.1) = 2\times1.5 + 5\times0.2 + 3\times1.1 = 3 + 1 + 3.3 = 7.3\ \$\). Each product is (items) × (dollars per item) = dollars, so the three terms can be added.

Exercise 7.3. Find the angle between \((4, -2)\) and \((1, 2)\).

Show answer

Dot product \(4\times1 + (-2)\times2 = 4 - 4 = 0\). So \(\cos\theta = 0\) and \(\theta = 90^\circ\). The lengths (\(\sqrt{20}\) and \(\sqrt5\)) do not matter once the dot product is zero.

Exercise 7.4. Compute \(\det\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\). What happens to the area of a 2 × 2 square, and to its orientation?

Show answer

\(1\times1 - 3\times2 = 1 - 6 = -5\). Areas are multiplied by \(\lvert -5\rvert = 5\), so the 2 × 2 square (area 4) becomes a parallelogram of area 20. The negative sign means orientation flips: corners that went anticlockwise now go clockwise.

Exercise 7.5. Using the bakery matrix \(R\) and its inverse from 7.8, the bakery used 4.6 kg of flour and 16 eggs. How many cakes did it bake? Then find the number of loaves, and check by multiplying back.

Show answer

\(R^{-1}(4.6, 16) = (2\times4.6 - 0.15\times16,\ 0\times4.6 + 0.25\times16) = (9.2 - 2.4,\ 4) = (6.8, 4)\). So 4 cakes and 6.8 loaves. (A real bakery would not bake 0.8 of a loaf; the numbers are made up to be clean.) Check: \(R(6.8, 4) = (0.5\times6.8 + 0.3\times4,\ 4\times4) = (3.4 + 1.2, 16) = (4.6, 16)\).

Exercise 7.6. Let \(Q\) be the quarter turn and \(D\) the horizontal stretch from 7.6. Compute both \(DQ\) and \(QD\) entry by entry. Apply each to \(\mathbf{e}_1\) and compare with the two results in the text. What are \(\det(DQ)\) and \(\det(QD)\), and why must they be equal?

Show answer

\(DQ = \begin{pmatrix} 2\cdot0 + 0\cdot1 & 2\cdot(-1) + 0\cdot0 \\ 0\cdot0 + 1\cdot1 & 0\cdot(-1) + 1\cdot0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -2 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\); first column \((0, 1)\), matching “\(Q\) then \(D\)”. \(QD = \begin{pmatrix} 0\cdot2 + (-1)\cdot0 & 0\cdot0 + (-1)\cdot1 \\ 1\cdot2 + 0\cdot0 & 1\cdot0 + 0\cdot1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 2 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\); first column \((0, 2)\), matching “\(D\) then \(Q\)”. Both determinants are \(0\cdot0 - (-2)\cdot1 = 2\) and \(0\cdot0 - (-1)\cdot2 = 2\). They must agree because \(\det(DQ) = \det D\det Q = 2\times1\) in either order: area scaling factors are plain numbers and commute, even though the maps do not.

Exercise 7.7. First project \(\mathbf{v} = (4, 2)\) onto the line through \(\mathbf{u} = (2, 1)\). What is special about the answer? Then project \(\mathbf{v} = (4, 0)\) onto the same line. Give the first component of the second projection.

Show answer

First: \((4, 2) = 2(2, 1)\) is already on the line, so the projection is \((4, 2)\) itself and the residual is \(\mathbf{0}\). Second: \(\hat{\mathbf{u}} = (2, 1)/\sqrt5\), \(\mathbf{v}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{u}} = 8/\sqrt5\), projection \(= (8/\sqrt5)(2,1)/\sqrt5 = (8/5)(2, 1) = (3.2, 1.6)\). Residual \((0.8, -1.6)\); check \((0.8, -1.6)\cdot(2,1) = 1.6 - 1.6 = 0\).

Exercise 7.8. Let \(\mathbf{a} = (1, -1)\) and \(\mathbf{b} = (2, 1)\). Write out \(\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^\top\). Find a nonzero input it sends to \(\mathbf{0}\), and the output for \(\mathbf{v} = (1, 1)\). What is its rank, and why can no choice of \(\mathbf{v}\) produce the output \((1, 1)\)?

Show answer

\(\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^\top = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ -2 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\). Any \(\mathbf{v}\) perpendicular to \(\mathbf{b}\) is sent to zero, for example \((1, -2)\): \(2 - 2 = 0\) and \(-2 + 2 = 0\). For \(\mathbf{v} = (1,1)\): \(s = 2 + 1 = 3\), output \(3(1, -1) = (3, -3)\). Rank 1: every output is a multiple of \(\mathbf{a} = (1, -1)\). The vector \((1, 1)\) is not a multiple of \((1, -1)\), so it is never written.

Exercise 7.9. A symmetric \(d \times d\) matrix is fixed by its diagonal and the entries above it. How many numbers is that for \(d = 5{,}120\)? (This counts the independent entries of one Hessian block at Level 15.)

Show answer

Diagonal: \(d\) entries. Above the diagonal: \(d(d - 1)/2\). Total \(d + d(d-1)/2 = d(d+1)/2 = 5{,}120 \times 5{,}121 / 2 = 26{,}219{,}520 / 2 = 13{,}109{,}760\).
